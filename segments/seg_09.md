# Chapter XII

Chapter argument: The necessity of the Babylonian captivity for the reformation of the church; the heads of the Ezraitic reformation; the great synagogue restored; its origin and work; the feast of tabernacles restored, pleasing to God; the single rule of ecclesiastical reformation; the repudiation of polygamy [Greek: polygamia]; the accuracy of the biblical text; the origin of the Hebrew points.

I. That God did not cast off all care of the church when it was carried away into captivity was both evident from the event itself, and he had himself given the godly assurance of it by his promises. Nay rather, to that most merciful Father that brief deportation [Greek: metoikēsia] was punishment enough for the greatest of crimes. Far different was the condition of that people while the captivity lasted from the condition of the same people now, cut off from God in the most absolute separation. For at that time they were destitute neither of the written word, nor of the Holy Spirit, nor of the prophetic ministry, nor of a most certain hope of return. But, as we have said, it was impossible for that church to be recovered to any good fruit without a total interruption of its solemn observances. For since the temple was holy, the city holy, and the whole land holy; since the priests all sprang from one fixed family, and the sacred administrations were bound to a single place; and since the greatest part of the priests and of the people was corrupt and clung obstinately to its apostate defection, a general removal from the seats of the church had of necessity to be set in train as the preparation for reformation. And when the appointed time of the captivity had run its course, the returning people, under the leadership of godly princes, [ p. 347 ] priests, and prophets, undertook a most complete reformation of the church according to the rule of the Mosaic theology, as the historical books of Ezra and Nehemiah and the prophetic books of Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi bear witness.

II. Of that reformation, which from its principal author we shall call the Ezraitic, there were six more solemn parts, namely: 1. The restoration of the temple; 2. The institution of the great synagogue; 3. A most diligent examination of the copies of the sacred books; 4. The sedulous preaching of the word of God; 5. The solemn excommunication of the Cuthaeans; 6. Separation from the mixed people.

III. What pertains to the restoration of the temple, since it has been more than sufficiently belabored by others on account of certain chronological difficulties, and does not properly belong to our design, we have thought fit to pass over.

IV. As the second part of the Ezraitic reformation we set down the institution of the great synagogue, which is remembered with celebrated fame among the Jews. "The men of the great synagogue" [Hebrew: anshei keneset ha-gedolah] is the most customary periphrasis for those reformers. Whoever wishes to know more of it may consult, besides the Jews, Martinus Raymundus, Pugio Fidei, part ii. cap. iv.; Petrus Galatinus, Arcana, lib. iv. cap. vi.; Bodin, De Republica, lib. vi. cap. viii.; Baronius, Annales, tom. i. A.C. xxxi. 10, 11, xxxiii. 19; Lorinus on Numbers xi. 16; Casaubon, Exercitationes, xiii. 5; Cappellus, Vindiciae pro Casaubono, lib. iii. cap. ii.; Cunaeus, De Republica Hebraeorum; Constantine l'Empereur on the tractate Middoth, cap. v. sec. 3.

V. A sanhedrim of seventy-one men had been instituted by Moses in the wilderness, which, in comparison with the lesser assemblies of judges, is commonly called the great. Some affirm that it served only for the age of the wilderness wandering, and did not continue when judges or kings were established in the land of Canaan; but that these men are egregiously mistaken is shown by Selden, that keenest vindicator of these sanhedrims, who nevertheless does not deny that they were many times intermitted. This sanhedrim the returning people restored in the first place. There are, I confess, those whose stomachs cannot bear this most eminent part of the Ezraitic reformation, and who hold what is said about it for Jewish figments; while they themselves stand ready to believe whatever the lying Talmud dares to invent in its traditions, provided only it turns to the advantage of the opinions they cherish. But, as we have said, the origin of this synod of seventy-one was sacred, proceeding from the express command of God. And those who dream that those great men and prophets returned from the captivity, men most learned in the law of God and burning with zeal for a reformation of every kind, did not restore that assembly, without which they could preserve neither order nor authority, will not have me for a rival. For they are not found to have been so wanting to their duty in other matters that we should suspect them of negligence in this institution of the highest utility, and of necessity for the government of the people.
VI. Further: that assembly of reformers was called the "great synagogue" preeminently [Greek: kat' exochēn], not from the number of its members, which was precisely determined, but on account of the dignity of the persons who composed it. [ p. 348 ] For very many of its members were men divinely inspired [Greek: theopneustoi], prophets, and amanuenses of the Holy Spirit. An assembly of that kind the church had never seen since the days of Moses; and therefore it ought to seem strange to no one if it brought back into sacred practice things which, though instituted by God, had lain despised and neglected almost from the very beginnings of the church; and if, relying on divine authority, it made certain determinations concerning the sacred Scripture itself, for the use and benefit of all the faithful to the end of the world. The reformation of a church comes nearest to its first institution. It was therefore necessary that all those prophets and divine men should be, as it were, one Moses; for a great and arduous work lay upon them, by which the ruins of many generations were to be repaired. And it pleases me to add, by one or two examples, a specimen of their supreme diligence in restoring all things exactly to the rule of the Mosaic theology.

VII. Let the first be the restoration of the feast of tabernacles, Neh. 8:14, 16-18: "When they found written in the law that Jehovah had commanded by Moses that the Israelites should dwell in booths in their feast of the seventh month... The people went out... and made themselves booths, every one upon his roof, or in their courts, and in the courts of the house of God, and in the street of the water gate. And when all that congregation of those returned from the captivity had thus made booths, and sat in those booths (for the children of Israel had not done so from the days of Joshua the son of Nun until that day), there was very great gladness." That is, although they had observed the feast of the seventh month, they had not sat in booths since the days of Joshua. But what need was there, you will say, to recall into use an antiquated institution, which Samuel the prophet, David the king, and Josiah, the greatest of the reformers before the captivity, had omitted? Was it not mere and excessive curiosity, in a reformation, to look back beyond the times of David, in which the religious observance of divine worship most especially flourished? But not so did it seem to God. For to testify that this duty of theirs was grateful and acceptable to him, he forthwith by the prophet not only extols that feast with great praises, but moreover celebrates under its name the spiritual worship of the gospel that was to be introduced, Zech. 14:16-19; namely, that the godly reformers might learn from this of how great price it was with God to attend strictly and accurately, all other considerations laid aside, to the rule of the Mosaic theology in their work.
VIII. That this synagogue also banished from the borders of the church the unlawful mingling [Greek: athemiton mixin] with the Gentiles, which from the days of Solomon had scarcely been piously avoided, we shall presently show; but they ascended higher still. For these men, furnished with the spirit of prophecy, set themselves against a most ancient custom, never approved by the true God, of taking several wives, and of dismissing for the most trifling causes those whom they had joined to themselves by lawful covenants, a custom which had prevailed among the whole nation from ancient times [Greek: ap' archaiōn], Mal. 2:14-16: "Jehovah has been witness between you and the wife of your youth, against whom you deal treacherously, though she is your companion and the wife of your covenant. For did he not [ p. 349 ] make one, though the residue of the spirit was with him? And why one? That he might seek a seed of God... He hates putting away, says Jehovah of Israel." An illustrious testimony against polygamy, such as exists nowhere else in the whole Old Testament before the captivity. I confess the former words, ver. 15, are ambiguous, "and did not one make" [Hebrew: ve-lo echad asah], which interpreters render variously: "Did not one make?" "Did he not make one?" The Greek has "And did not another make?" [Greek: kai ouk allos epoiēse]. For since echad is of the masculine gender, it seems unable to denote a woman. But that word has no feminine form derived from it. For achat ["one," feminine] is not from echad, which would have produced achedet, but from ach, as Rabbi David Kimchi teaches in his Michlol. It is no wonder, then, if echad is sometimes employed in the feminine gender, inasmuch as by the force of its acceptation it signifies neutrally, Zech. 11:7; and the following words, moreover, admit no other sense. The Targum of Jonathan trifles with some nonsense about the one Abraham. But the words must manifestly be interpreted either of the single woman created at the beginning, as their author himself and the best interpreter of Scripture, our Lord Jesus Christ, seems to render their sense; or of the counsel of the one God, joining one male and one female in lawful matrimony.

IX. The words also in which the prophet treats of divorces are not free from all ambiguity, ver. 16: "for he hates putting away, says Jehovah" [Hebrew: ki sane shallach amar Yehovah]. Jerome: "When you hate her, put her away, says the Lord." The old English version: "If he hate her, let him put her away;" which reading the royal translation retains in the margin, though it interprets the words in a plainly different sense, "The Lord saith that he hateth putting away," according to the mind of the Holy Spirit. For since the words are set down elliptically, that sense is to be embraced which they most nearly yield without arbitrary supplements. The word shallach ["putting away"] is therefore put absolutely, and God testifies that he holds that putting away in hatred.
X. In this manner were the first foundations laid of the reformation of the Jewish church returned from captivity. Searching out with the utmost fidelity and diligence the very original institutions of God, however antiquated through so many ages and consigned to oblivion, they adhered to them most religiously.
XI. The accuracy bestowed upon the sacred Scripture we mentioned in the next place. That is the greatest treasure of the church, to which, under the care of God and the administration of the Spirit, it owes its existence on earth. This was the single law of the whole reformation, and therefore it was to be attended to with accuracy in the first place. That Ezra and his prophetic companions had with them ancient copies preserved with the highest fidelity, no one can justly doubt. They devoted their supreme labor to transcribing these most correctly, and to removing from others the faults which it is not impossible could have crept in during the dispersion. Some ascribe to the collation of all the copies the beginning of the Keri and Kethib [Hebrew: qere u-khethib, the "read" and "written" marginal variants]. And in the whole work they enjoyed the most immediate inspiration and guidance of God. But that Ezra neither wished, nor ought, nor was able, nor had any need [ p. 350 ] to change the ancient letters in which the amanuenses of the Holy Spirit, the prophets, had written the sacred word after the pattern of those which God himself first engraved, we have proved before. There is also another figment which, otherwise than was fitting, has gained credit with some great men: namely, that the whole sacred Scripture was lost, and was restored by Ezra from memory and committed to writing. A most absurd lie, and a blasphemy at once! God forbid that anyone hereafter should cast this reproach upon his care, providence, and love toward the church!
XII. Very learned men are of the opinion that the invention of the Hebrew punctation [the vowel points] belongs to the Ezraitic accuracy bestowed upon the biblical text. I accede to their judgment so far as to count it most highly probable. For after the prophetic ministry ceased in the Jewish church, it is a matter of religious scruple to suppose that any others whatsoever undertook that work. Whether, therefore, we say that the vowels were coeval with the letters themselves, or devised by Ezra, it comes to the same thing, since either opinion ascribes to them a divine origin. But there are those who determine the whole Hebrew punctation to be a rabbinical, post-Talmudic invention [Greek: heurēma]. What I think in this cause, since the plan of my undertaking requires it, I hope I may bring forward without offense to anyone, here or elsewhere; although another attempt of mine in this quarter did not succeed so prosperously, by which, all unwitting, I frightened off a hen clucking over her new-hatched chick. [A wry allusion to the controversy stirred by Owen's earlier treatise against Brian Walton's Polyglot prolegomena on this question.]
# A Digression on the Origin of the Hebrew Punctation

I. It is the opinion of great men that Ezra and his colleagues, the assessors of the great synagogue, while they were bestowing their care upon the sacred Scriptures, invented the Hebrew punctation, since before that time the Jews used in writing only the twenty-two consonant letters. For my part, I have no wish to gainsay the opinion of those who maintain that the vowels were coeval with the letters, nor yet to persist obstinately in it; since even those who hold that the origin of the points is to be ascribed to Ezra assert that it was plainly divine. That the Hebrew punctation, then, which by the goodness of God we now use, is to be assigned to no one after the times of Ezra, is the position whose defense we undertake in this digression; or, what comes to the same thing, that it had a divine and infallible origin. For there are some who suppose that certain post-Talmudic rabbis devised it; nor do they merely suppose it, but they think those who judge otherwise deserve to be received with insults. But the monstrous confidence which they employ does not at all deter me from subjecting their opinion to examination. For whoever has not learned to despise, or at least [ p. 351 ] to disregard, the angry passions and the foot-and-a-half-long words which some men, as though pleading the cause of truth, put forward from minds puffed up with disdain and excessive self-opinion, or swollen with the leaven of party zeal, will find it necessary in these festering times to cast away all hope and care of defending the truth.
II. When learned men first parted ways into this division of opinions, it seemed truly a matter of very little moment however the dispute might be settled. Elias Levita is almost the only one among the Jews who inclines to the opinion of the novelty of the punctation. But he, since he held that it had always been a part of the oral law, that shadow or cloud for which that most superstitious nation pines away, and was only committed to writing by the rabbis, is untroubled about its divine authority. Perhaps, indeed, it was all one to him whether it be reckoned to have proceeded from God himself or from the masters, who all but occupy the place of God. The Christians who first embraced that opinion could not foresee in their thoughts what evils would take their rise from it. But now that the audacity of certain men in profaning the sacred Scriptures has ceased to be tolerable, and advances daily, lest at last nothing unshaken [Greek: akinēton], nothing sacred, be left in sacred things, it seems to concern all who are held by a due reverence for the divine word to guard most religiously every tittle of the Hebrew verity. For from this one error concerning the rabbinical and arbitrary invention of the Hebrew points, we see that very many evils have poured themselves out upon the church of God, threatening destruction to most holy truth. Hence some affirm that the single words of sacred Scripture, apart from all connection with others, ought to be considered and weighed as so many hieroglyphic signs, if you please; others, that the sacred Scriptures have been corrupted by the Jews in that disposition or fixing of the points which we now use; among these is Bellarmine, De Verbo Dei, lib. ii. cap. ii. Johannes Morinus contends that the sense of the Hebrew text cannot be perceived by us without the help of the points; and that therefore, since the points owe their origin to the labor of uncertain men, fallible in all things and in some openly false, that Hebrew text cannot be the rule and norm of faith, Exercitationes, lib. i. cap. ii. Gregory de Valentia accordingly wishes it to be corrected to the exemplar of the Vulgate Latin version, tom. i. disput. v. quaest. 3. Another, whom for honor's sake and for other reasons I do not name, holds that this rabbinical invention [Greek: heurēma] is not so perfect but that it could be made better. Another judges that the punctation may be changed by anyone at his pleasure, provided a more convenient sense of the words be elicited by that change. Cappellus adds that very many differences between the Hebrew text and the ancient versions, especially the Greek of the Seventy, took their rise from this, that that version was made before the invention of the points, and accordingly followed that reading which was then in use, from which the Masoretes in their punctation many times departed. And I fear that, this door once opened, still greater monstrosities of opinion may be expected from the curious and itching wits of this age, especially if diligence be added to audacity. How this dispute was agitated not long since among learned men, especially between Buxtorf and Cappellus, is hidden from no one [ p. 352 ] who cares to know these things. What has lately been said by certain others is almost all borrowed from them. Nay, I am well enough assured that some would by no means have marched forth so thickly hedged about with testimonies of the rabbis for the novelty of the points, had they not plowed so far with the heifer of the most distinguished Buxtorf as to convert to their own use what he had brought forward by way of objection against himself.
III. It is now two years and somewhat more since I wrote and published in our vernacular tongue certain things on this question of the origin of the points. These a certain person received in such a manner as becomes a man who feels it to belong to his honor to display the greatest contempt for others. It would be easy to strip that little crow of the borrowed colors, relying on which he arrogates everything to himself, if that were the business now in hand. But our present design does not suffer it. Nor would the work before us, swelling to an unexpected bulk, permit me to pursue in this place everything that bears on the unraveling of this question and the deciding of the dispute, as I purposed at the outset. Briefly touching, therefore, the weight of the reasons on which I rely in still persevering in the opinion of the divine origin of the Hebrew punctation, and subjecting to examination the chief objections by which some strive to assail it, I shall press on to what remains to be said of the Ezraitic reformation.
IV. First, then, we argue from universal use and possession. The possession of so great a treasure is not lightly to be abandoned. The whole church of God holds this punctation of the Scriptures of the Old Testament as her own peculiar property. The adversaries of our opinion will not deny that a great benefit is lodged in it. Nowhere among the nations does there exist a monument of any copy older than the punctation. No legitimate prescription, therefore, can be brought against the possession. Whatever is said on the adverse side is conjecture. It is indeed true that there are very many copies, and that there formerly existed, from all antiquity backwards, copies written without points. But that none exists coeval with the time at which the patrons of the novelty of the points pretend they were invented, they themselves, I think, will confess. The possession, therefore, is legitimate, and the prescription duly established by antiquity. Among the books of Selden which are now preserved in the Bodleian Library there is a most ancient Pentateuch, most beautifully written out in two volumes, with verses alternately in Hebrew and Chaldee, so that the Targum everywhere adheres to the text. At the first glance at the book I immediately perceived that the vowel points had been added and inserted long after the writing of the letters. The manner of the writing and the color of the ink bear witness to it. But that this codex was written only some centuries after the pretended rabbinical invention of the punctation can be shown by very many indications. Now although this argument is not of itself strong enough to settle this whole dispute, yet if the weightiest considerations of reason are not produced on the contrary side, it will be found to have strength enough and to spare for repelling objections. Whoever will concede that grammatical trifles, the silence of one or two ancient writers about the points, sophistical quibbles, most uncertain conjectures, and other things [ p. 353 ] of that kind, suffice to eject the church of God from this possession, is, if I am any judge, too easy in a cause not his own. The arguments, therefore, which are wont to be brought forward to overthrow the divine origin of the points are not to be weighed in an equal balance, inclining neither this way nor that, against those which fight for it; since on this side there hangs also the weight of this possession, which must first be moved from its place before the adverse reasons can be reckoned to advance on an equal footing. In other affairs there are few who labor under such sloth and negligence as to suffer themselves to be so easily circumvented and expelled from their hereditary goods. Let every man do as he will; for my part, assuredly, I shall not yield up this possession except unwillingly, and overborne by reasons.
V. 2. The very nature of the thing clearly shows that it is from heaven, not from men. It breathes everywhere something divine [Greek: theion ti]. From the beginning of the holy books to the end, like itself, consistent with itself, everywhere sincerely and infallibly determining the sense of the letters, this punctation points out and declares the mind of the Holy Spirit. The men of the opposing party [Greek: hoi ex enantias] will never to all eternity produce one objection [Greek: enstasis] to the contrary that is of any weight. This shines forth most of all in the most obscure prophecies, those that conceal the profoundest mysteries, in the understanding of which all the Jews are blinder than moles, and always have been since the coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. Grave and learned men, I hope, would not wish to sell the light which the punctation brings to innumerable places of sacred Scripture at the price of most uncertain conjectures, were there not present a disproportion in the counter-pull [Greek: ametria antholkēs]. As for the claims that certain accents are superfluous and useless, that the silent sheva is often added in vain and multiplied without cause, and quibbling subtleties [Greek: leptologiai] of that sort, which Cappellus hints at in his reformation of the grammar in support of his hypothesis, they are so frivolous and inept that I marvel that learned men could insist on them or ever bring their minds to entertain them. Learned men will not say, I think, that they rightly grasp the reason of every single point in the sacred Scriptures: certainly I know who they are that have told me, in their writings, that is, and published books, that certain marks in the Hebrew punctation are utterly useless, and the accents superfluous; but who has told them the same, I know not; nor in so great a cause am I minded to attach faith and authority to grammatical trifles and to the audacious conjectures of puffed-up men. Others say that the Hebrew punctation is not so perfect a work but that it can be corrected, amended, and rendered more perfect. There is more than one reason why I should judge these men too much elated with self-opinion, or knowingly and willingly carrying themselves more magnificently than their merits warrant, the more fastidiously to despise others. But come: if they are men, and so please, let them try their strength. We shall see at once how far "brass coins differ from lupine counters" (Horace, Epistles 1.7.23). Him, and the wisdom of him, who put forth the punctation, they will never equal, though they burst themselves in the attempt. Let them proceed by particular instances, or it will profit nothing to pour out empty words, of which "there is a wide range, this way and that" (Homer, Iliad 20.249). Whoever shall seriously weigh this work will find it perfect, divine, most absolute, like the other works of God, to which nothing can be added and nothing taken away. To the man who would amend or [ p. 354 ] correct it I will say, "Remember the battle, and do so no more" [Hebrew: zekhor milchamah al-tosaph] (Job 41:8). Nor indeed can I sufficiently wonder what came into the minds of learned men, that they should ascribe a work of this kind, to the amending of which in any particular the whole nation of the learned has always been, and even now is, unequal, to certain unlearned worthless fellows, destitute of all aids, both spiritual and of secular science.
VI. 3. In this question, which is one of fact, the testimonies of the Jews themselves are of no small weight. Here they all think the same: namely, that the origin of the punctation was divine. Even Elias Levita, who almost alone among his own people dared to ascribe the designation of the open sounds by little marks to the post-Talmudic Masoretes of Tiberias, nevertheless everywhere contends that it was a part of the oral law delivered by God himself on Mount Sinai, and preserved no less religiously and inviolately than if it had been committed to writing from its first origin. But very many of them, and the most learned, constantly assign that writing to Moses or to Ezra. Whoever wishes to see this established by testimonies, let him go to the most learned dissertation of the renowned Buxtorf on the origin of the Hebrew punctation, and to Joseph de Voysin in his most erudite preface to the Pugio Fidei of Martinus Raymundus. But when I had said elsewhere that the testimonies of the rabbis avail much in this cause, it was retorted that they ought therefore to be reckoned competent to invent the punctation itself. But the fatuity of that most unfair comparison betrays itself. For some men can testify that a thing was done divinely, or at least that neither they nor their fathers did it, who are in truth altogether unequal and unfit for the doing of the thing itself. But that they may mock the testimonies of the Hebrews, they say that the contention is not about the sounds of the vowels, but only about the signs. For they acknowledge that the Hebrews always had the same vowel sounds which they still use, and thus that the language was never destitute of vowels. Not long ago a certain person judged the observation of this distinction to be of such moment for the settling of this whole dispute that he fell upon me with unbridled temper, because, in recounting this controversy, I had thought no account need be taken of it. But I could not suspect that the question at issue was whether all the Hebrews were utterly dumb down to the time of the Tiberian Masoretes. For if they were destitute of vowel sounds, they were also dumb, and could never pronounce one complete word. For that nothing can be uttered by the letters themselves without the open sounds of the vowels is known even to those "who are not yet washed in the bath for a penny" [that is, to mere children]. Whoever, therefore, shall strive to prove by reasons and arguments that in this sense the Hebrew language had vowels, not only before it came into anyone's mind to express them by marks or figures, but even before a single consonant letter was written or engraved, has without doubt got himself a cause in which "it is easy to be eloquent." Meanwhile it is the origin of the points, not of the sounds, that we are investigating. But since the patrons of the novelty of the points judge it to concern their cause that this distinction between the sounds and the figures of the vowels be diligently weighed in the agitation of this [ p. 355 ] question, come, let us concede that the Hebrews before the invention of the points were not dumb, but spoke after the manner of other men. What they can carve out of that for the support of their cause, however, we have still to wait and see.
VII. 4. The supreme utility, if not the absolute necessity, of the points and accents for the right understanding of the sacred text, since I have set it forth elsewhere, I do not wish to relate here at greater length. I leave these matters to the judgment of all who have ever seriously employed the Hebrew language in the study of the sacred letters. Rabbi Bechai brings forward to this end an excellent testimony from the book Bahir, on the section Beshallach, Exod. 13:17: "The points stand to the letters of the law of Moses as the soul of life stands in the body of a man." But if that heavenly light, which accrues to the truth from the fixing and restricting of the sense of the words which the Holy Spirit uses, be reckoned the product of human wit and discretion, how great evils will at once arise from thence, and what prejudices to the faith, no one can easily conjecture. The memory of the attempts of certain men, which that supposition has spawned, is exceedingly unwelcome. Hence we have seen some men, without horror and without the most just indignation, parading the uncertainty and obscurity of the Hebrew text, the institution of its correction by means of the versions, and even by conjectures, if only a man will use them modestly, in the judgment, forsooth, of those very men who, that they may show themselves skilled craftsmen in the trimming up of learned conjectures, stand ready to cast off all reverence for God and men, while they lean on conjectures that are doubtful, uncertain, nay, utterly inept. And the itching and ambitious wits of this age compel us to dread things worse still. Let the vowel points be removed, and all the distinguishing accents; let the bare letters remain, or rather letters thus stripped bare, with no distinction applied throughout any book, each word divided from the rest by some point, as among the Samaritans, or at least by an imaginary one, and it would at once be evident what an Iliad of evils, what scandals and perils, would inundate the church. And yet, if the whole punctation be the invention [Greek: heurēma] of uncertain men, of Jewish rabbis, no reason of any weight can be brought forward why all these things might not be so removed and cast aside, nay, perhaps, why they ought not to be.
But they say that those letters aleph, vav, yod, which they call mothers of reading [matres lectionis], together with he, were before the invention of the points either vowels or employed in the place of vowels, by whose help readers could easily remedy all those inconveniences which seem to attend the absence of vowels; and that the points were not affixed by the Tiberian Masoretes arbitrarily or at their pleasure, and that therefore the punctation does not rest upon their authority. But I, in the first place, would gladly know whether those letters which they call mothers of reading were employed to that end only as they now appear in the sacred text, or whether they were also added and inserted in all the other places in which the sound of the word demands their presence. If they shall say that only those were employed of old which still adhere to the text, and in those places and words in which they persist, [ p. 356 ] it is clearer than the noonday light that they could not supply the office of vowels nor make good their lack; since there are innumerable words in which they nowhere appear, and sometimes, where they are present, they do not rightly serve the pronunciation, if any faith at all is to be placed in the present punctation; for it would be necessary that the single letter aleph should by itself render almost all the open sounds, and everyone sees how ridiculous it would be to imagine that. But if they shall say that these vowel letters were of old multiplied in the text, and written everywhere according to the rule of the sound of the words, so that, for example, davar ["word"] was written d-aleph-b-aleph-r, I should like to know who they were who dared to pluck out and cast away so many thousands of letters written in the sacred Scriptures by God himself and the prophets, or when we are to suppose that this was done. The religion, or the superstition, of the Jews alike forbids us to ascribe this audacious crime to them. It is not the part of the same men to keep a scrupulously exact account of how often every single letter is employed in the whole Bible, and to throw away so many thousands of their own accord. This figment, therefore, will not heal the wound which the newfangled opinion of the novelty of the points inflicts upon the light of truth and the perspicuity of the Scriptures. And as for the further pretense that Origen expressed the Hebrew text in Greek characters by the help of these vowel letters, it is established by no reasons or testimonies. It is not fair that any place should be assigned in so great a cause to bare affirmations, suspicions, and conjectures. He could learn the pronunciation of the Hebrew language from the usage of the Jews, or from the points themselves; so that for representing the Hebrew text in Greek characters he had no need of that fictitious and most imperfect aid.
Next, as to the other part of the answer set down above, that the Tiberian Masoretes did not point the Scriptures at their own discretion: the Jews, relying on their futile tradition of the oral law, can put this forward with some modesty and show of probability; not so others, those, namely, who acknowledge that oral law to be a figment of superstitious men. Yet there is one who, in his defense of the novelty of the points, asserts twenty times over, and not without insults against those of the contrary mind, that those Masoretes pointed the Scripture according to the received reading, which duly exhibited the mind of the Holy Spirit, and not at their own discretion. Let us see, then, what sound reason lies beneath this, to persuade anyone that this pretended punctation of the Masoretes is to be held of any value. "The Masoretes," they say, "affixed the points to the Bible according to the reading then received and in use." But among whom, I ask, was that reading received which is set up as the norm of the affixing of the points? Among those very Masoretes, of course. For whether at the time when they say this deed was done there were any Christians who understood the Hebrew text is uncertain. The Masoretes, therefore, pointed the Bible according to the reading received among themselves. That is the hope we are given. Although we have shown elsewhere that they were the worst of worthless fellows, if such men there were, perpetually employing the audacity of feigning whatever they pleased, yet be it so, that in this undertaking it suited them to be honest. [ p. 357 ] What then follows? "That accepted reading, to which they attended in affixing the points, exhibited the genuine sense of the Holy Spirit." But of that sense, pray, who were the judges and arbiters? The Masoretes themselves, without doubt. And so the Masoretes affixed the points not at their own discretion, but according to the reading received among them and the sense which they judged best and true. If I were to deny this, how, pray, would my adversary prove it? Will he show that they acted in good faith in other matters? He never will. Will he produce witnesses? He has none; and Seneca teaches us that no one ever exacted an oath from a historian. Let us deal, then, by what is fair and good, and concede what cannot be proved. But in truth these things are not mutually repugnant: they could use their own judgment in discerning the true reading and in investigating the sense of the Holy Spirit, while nevertheless they had no form or norm of punctation, and were thus arbitrary judges of the work they undertook; unless no one is to be reckoned to act at his own discretion except he act perversely. Marcus Tullius, indeed, in his oration for Roscius, affirms that strict judgment is one thing and arbitration another. But the laws would never have appointed arbitrators if it were necessary that whoever determines anything at his discretion should determine it without judgment. We ourselves, surely, when we commit anything to the arbitration of others, do not intend that in pronouncing sentence upon us and our affairs they should indulge their own inclinations with no care for what is fair and right. When, therefore, I said that the Masoretes affixed the points to the Bible at their discretion, I understood nothing else, and could understand nothing else, than that they completed that work using and trusting their own judgments. The knowledge, then, the skill, the judgment, and the integrity of the Tiberian rabbis, all of which I would not purchase at the price of a rotten nut, are, according to this opinion, the sole foundation of the present reading of all Hebrew codices. For there is no mortal who has learned the use of the copies which even now are written without points, except by the help of the points. But, you say, relying on tradition, not on their own judgment, they exhibited in that punctation of theirs the reading received from the fathers. But how faithful guardians of traditions they were, how expert patchers-together and vendors of the most inept fables, nay, of horrendous blasphemies, under the name of most ancient traditions, as though they had counted it their glory to have utterly overturned every vestige of the truth, I do not wish to say at greater length here, since the matter is manifest and will be spoken of hereafter. From that tradition, then, since they themselves were constituted by their own authority, if not the dispensers, yet the guardians and conservators of it, we are not yet delivered from having to lean upon the good faith of one of those worthless fellows for the reading of the whole Hebrew text. But they say further, that the true reading of the text was preserved, after that language ceased to be vernacular, not so much by oral tradition as by the very writing of the text, which always remained entire. But this too seems strange to me; for it does not admit of any easy explanation how the true reading can be preserved for the writing by that written thing which cannot be read. For I would gladly know, since letters cannot be uttered without [ p. 358 ] the use of the open sounds or vowels, and may signify anything according to the pleasure of him who joins those sounds to them, how the distinction of the vowels and their legitimate use can depend upon them. But it does not please me to dwell longer on these things, lest I fall in again with disputants of that sort, who would hold that the pernicious consequences which the rabbinical invention of the points, whose patronage they have undertaken, would bring in, are to be imputed to those who are assured of their divine origin.
VIII. 5. Furthermore: strength and authority accrue to the opinion of the ancient and sacred origin of the points from the uncertainty and wavering of the authors of the contrary opinion in assigning their rise. They will not have it that Ezra invented the points; who then was the author of the work? When, and where in the world, did he live? To what end did he undertake the work? At whose command, persuasion, or authority? What taskmaster or taskmasters [Greek: ergodiōktai] of the work did he have, what overseers, what judges? What did those whose interest it chiefly was that the work be carried through exactly think or determine concerning the work undertaken and finished? All these things they either openly profess themselves ignorant of, or it is easy to prove it. It is said to have been the common work of the Tiberian Masoretes. So, to be sure, said Elias Levita. Some determine that it was completed in the fifth century after the birth of Christ, others the sixth, Morinus the seventh or eighth. But what if I should say that those Tiberian Masoretes never existed at all, and that the authors of this opinion, in order to feign that the points were devised by those Masoretes, first feigned the Masoretes themselves? Would they convict me of falsehood out of any approved history, out of the monuments of past events? I know well enough they cannot. I confess indeed that about the year 350, when Jerome was in Palestine, the Jews had an obscure and inglorious school in the city of Tiberias. Whether they continued in that place down to the time to which the contrivers and builders of this imaginary work are obliged to assign it is uncertain, and even improbable. For such was the condition of the nation at that time, that nowhere within the bounds of the Roman empire, which they had so often shaken with slaughters, dissensions, and wars, could they long subsist in safety in any great number. In that very place, moreover, where they are said to have completed this work, they had not long before suffered a great defeat at the hands of Gallus, the legate of the emperor Constantius. From that time forward it is utterly uncertain whether any vestige of a Masoretic school remained at Tiberias. Furthermore: everything in which the Jews ever had any learning or science was at that time cultivated more diligently in the east, around Babylon, where they had occupied more peaceful seats, than in Palestine. When the western rabbis had now for a century or two been almost as mute as fishes, there came forth from the east that great and most consummate work of fables and trifles, the Talmud which is called Babylonian. That a few obscure, ignoble manikins, of no name or worth, should have undertaken and perfected so great a work, without the knowledge and without the counsel of the celebrated men of the same profession, is for those to believe who hold their faith easy and ready at hand for everything that makes for the advantage of their cause. Furthermore: [ p. 359 ] whoever has saluted any Jewish writings even from the threshold knows that all Jews are, and ever since their final rejection have been, the most insolent trumpeters of the deeds done, as they imagine, gloriously by themselves and their people. So far have they always been from silently passing over true things in this arena, that they bring forth everywhere falsehoods, fictions, things unheard of, lying most impudently. Yet now, concerning this most divine invention, to which that nation never produced anything like or equal, there is the deepest silence among them for several centuries. After a thousand years, at length, came forth one Elias, who suspects that certain Tiberian Masoretes devised this work. Was there no one who could have committed to writing, for posterity, the origin of that divine artifice among the inventors themselves, especially when its glory and utility were so great that nowhere in the world has there been found Christian or Jew, learned or unlearned, who either wished or dared to gainsay its use or its perfection, in any part, or in the whole, or in the smallest point? Is it credible that the fame of so great an endeavor, exulting in prosperous success, utterly perished within the space of a brief age among a nation most retentive of all traditions, even about futile things and things of naught? I confess I do not have so much credulity in my store as to lend faith to monstrous conjectures of this kind.
IX. That the punctation of the Bible was a most excellent invention, no one, I think, will deny. It must plainly be said that nothing like it would ever arise elsewhere, and nothing like it ever has arisen. In its use the church has received an immortal benefit. There are learned men who determine that it is absolutely and indispensably necessary to the true understanding of the sacred letters. "Without the points," says Marcus Marinus in his preface to his Hebrew Lexicon, "no certain doctrine concerning this language can be delivered; since everything can be read in different ways, so that the confusion of this one language would be greater than that of Babel." And Rudolphus Cevallerius [Antoine Rodolphe Chevallier], De Rudimentis Linguae Hebraicae, cap. 4: "As for what remains concerning the antiquity of the vowels and accents, I subscribe to the opinion of those who affirm that the Hebrew language, as the most absolute archetype [Greek: archetypon] of all others, was plainly written so from the beginning; since those who think otherwise not only render the authority of sacred Scripture doubtful, but in my judgment tear it up by the roots, because without vowels and the marks of distinction it would have nothing certain and firm." Of the same opinion is Franciscus Junius, Animadversions on Bellarmine, De Verbo Dei, lib. ii. cap. ii., where, out of Johannes Isaac, a Christian Jew, "he compares the man who undertakes to read the Scriptures without points to an unbridled horse [Greek: achalinōtos]." I will not repeat what Jerome said of the writings of Jovinian, after another had said it of those of Plautus, "no one will read these but the Sibyl;" but I may borrow that saying of the poet, "opinion is spread over all things" [Greek: dokos d' epi pasi tetyktai] (Xenophanes, fr. 34); so on every side do difficulties present themselves in the bare letters. And worthy of note is what Engelbertus Engles has in his preface to the lexicon of Valentinus Schindler. "There are," he says, "many most learned and distinguished men who, because certain rabbis of the Jews assert that the points were invented by men long after the twenty-two [ p. 360 ] letters of the alphabet, suppose that the vowels of the Hebrews did not exist at one and the same time with the consonants; but if the places of Scripture, a few out of many just now enumerated, be considered more attentively, it will become evident either that the points arose together with the twenty-two letters, or that the Scripture was formerly flexible like wax, now to this side, now to that. And if the points were invented by men, it follows necessarily that the Scripture in this our time has an authority not divine but human." Hence that saying of Aben Ezra, in his Balance of the Holy Tongue: "Whatever exposition is not according to the rule of the accents, do not consent to it, nor listen to it." He has similar things in other places; I shall add only what he has in his commentary on Exod. 20:1: "Know that the words are as it were bodies, and the accents as it were souls; and the body is to the soul as a garment."
X. The far greater part of the theologians who have been exercised in this arena think the same. Some of the papists, indeed, freely concede that the Hebrew text, the punctation removed, either cannot be understood at all, or only with difficulty and very imperfectly. Then, snatching most audaciously to their own purpose the concessions of certain great men, who in their writings, in passing and while engaged on other business, have pronounced an over-hasty judgment in this cause without weighing all that bears upon it, it is marvelous with what audacity they triumph over the sacred Hebrew verity, as useless and scarcely, or not even scarcely, intelligible. Nay, there are not wanting those who bring a grave accusation against the men who most firmly believe the divine origin of the punctation, because these assert that, on the opinion of those who hold its origin rabbinical, it could, and perhaps even ought, to be done away with; as if they were doing injury to the sacred Scriptures, and contriving that by which much of the perspicuity of the text would be lost. By the same reasoning some Socinian might say that I deny that Christ is to be adored with religious worship, because I deny that he should be so adored unless he were that supreme and only God, while the Socinian himself is fully persuaded that he is not that supreme God. In this, then, we have all learned men voting with us: namely, that the use of the vowel points and accents is of such moment for perceiving the true and genuine sense of sacred Scripture, that were they removed, almost everything would be thrown into marvelous confusion and rendered uncertain. It is indeed true that those who have attained the true reading of the text by the help of the punctation, and retain the knowledge of that sense which the pointed words and the passages distinguished by accents pour forth, can make much progress in the study of the Scriptures by the help of other means. But let us suppose that those points had never been devised; let us utterly cast away all the sense and understanding which by their benefit we have in any way drawn from the sacred Scriptures, whether immediately or through the mediating studies and labors of others; let us approach the bare letters empty of preconceived opinions: [ p. 361 ] we shall see at once into what difficulties, uncertainties, and darkness we are thrown. For when the discretion of every individual has been set up as the norm of the pronunciation of words, of the reading of sentences, and of all pauses and distinctions, who will prevent the whole nation of the learned from falling at once into inextricable and pernicious quarrels? That word of God, thrown open as a prize of contest, will be the one apple of strife [Greek: mēlon eridos]. Whether proud and luxuriant wits, swollen with the opinion of their own knowledge, or anxious for a reputation for learning, would leave anything in it unshaken [Greek: akinēton] and undoubted, is plainly uncertain; nay rather, that they would leave nothing of the kind is almost most certain. On this punctation depend all those means which we use in learning that language, and likewise very many helps to progress in the solid knowledge of it. All these being cast aside (not this or that conjugation, as pleases Cappellus, since the reasoning is the same for all), without whose help and benefit not even the Jews themselves, who are wholly given to the study of this language, would have anything certain to lean upon, there will most certainly be opened to eternal quarrels not so much a wide window as the main camp gate. For is there almost any man of somewhat more learning than common who would not be ashamed, and weary of his own sloth, if he could devise nothing new which, himself being judge and witness, would conduce to amending the true and genuine reading of sacred Scripture, and to rightly fixing and settling the distinctions of the sentences? And shall we suppose that no one would straightway be found who, to show himself a more excellent craftsman than the other, or more skilled in trimming up learned conjectures, would throw down the other's structure? He must be a stranger in all literary matters, especially critical ones, who thinks otherwise. But there is no need, they say, that the present reading or punctation be changed in anything. But, say I, for any work to be held so sacred that no one has the right to change it in anything, or to innovate anything in it, this must accrue to it either from the excellence of the work itself, or, beyond that, from the supreme and inviolable authority of its author. Where the excellence and contrivance of the work alone is pleaded, it is fair that that excellence and contrivance be accurately and diligently weighed, that it may appear they are not pleaded in vain. And the examination once instituted, it is wholly open and free to the examiner to reject what seems less congruent with right reason. And there is nothing to forbid its being done so in this cause. They say this device of the punctation is indeed most excellent, and therefore not lightly to be rejected in anything. Very well; but it is not exempted from free examination on account of its excellence; nothing less; nay, where by a change of it a reading truer in the judgment of the examiners, or a more convenient sense, can be elicited, since it holds its place only by merit, subject to examination, that change will be not only lawful but even necessary. And it is plain that this must be said of all sentences, nay, of every single word. Come then, let the critics try their strength, let them exhaust here their whole faculty of opining [Greek: doxastikē], let them cart hither their wagonloads of annotations, let them found new readings and new senses; and that most faithful guardian of sacred truth, namely reverence for the received [ p. 362 ] reading, "is gone, is lost, has taken ship" (a tag from Roman comedy). Many things, I confess, are brought forward by the patrons of the novelty of the points in answer to the argument drawn from the impossibility of learning that language, and of attaining the genuine sense of Scripture in many places, the benefit of the punctation being removed. Johannes Morinus confesses that the matter truly stands so, and that an accurate knowledge of this language is plainly impossible; and he laughs at those who suppose that the Masoretes in their punctation observed the correct pronunciation of the language. It is true that the Hebrew language, compared with other languages, is contained in very few words. But it is not thereby rendered easy or expeditious to learn. For although the words or terms are few, yet many are of the same signification; and there is scarcely one which does not admit several senses, and very many admit a multitude. The same letters exhibit various, often plainly diverse, significations. The case of dabar ["word," "thing," "pestilence," etc.] is well known. The letters of shelomoh yield "Solomon," and "perfect," and "recompense," and "garment." Innumerable examples of this kind may be sought from any vocabulary of this language, and from the concordances. While indeed it was vernacular, and was daily uttered by an innumerable multitude of men, it was not very difficult to perceive rightly the force of each word and to retain it by use. But when it ceased to be in common use, and there was no man to whom it was native and vernacular, and the whole of it was preserved in one book, it is plainly impossible that its correct pronunciation should be continued except from a consideration of the text of the book itself. Some urge that the Greek language formerly lacked accents, and yet was sufficiently open to the understanding. But whoever decides that the Greek accents are to be compared with the Hebrew points and accents seems not sufficiently to perceive the use or office of either in either language. The Greek language has such amplitude that almost everything abounds in it. Then, so great in it is the regular variation of words, and their mutual dependence in construction, that the accents conduce little or nothing to the understanding of a discourse. Very few are the words which, consisting of the same letters in certain tenses, are distinguished in sense by the accents. In Hebrew everything stands otherwise: the variation of the words is the least possible, the dependence in the series of a discourse rare and abrupt, the ellipses most frequent, the ambiguity of the words almost infinite. And yet even in Greek itself, where a word is truly of doubtful signification through the lack of an accent, which does not often happen, it is not hard to slip. Let an oversight [Greek: parorama] of the great Jerome serve as an example. Eusebius wrote in his Chronicle, at the year 1426: "Epimenides purifies Athens" [Greek: Epimenidēs tas Athēnas kathairei]. Jerome translated it, "Epimenides overthrew Athens," instead of "purified;" since it is doubtful whether kathairei should be read as "he destroys" [kathairei from kathaireō] or "he purifies" [kathairei from kathairō]; but the matter is known from history, for Epimenides did not overthrow the Attic land, but purified it with sacrifices. Again, as some think, the true sense of a proverbial saying escaped Aristotle himself, which he mentions in the third book of the Nicomachean Ethics, cap. v.: "No one is wicked willingly, nor blessed against his will" [Greek: Oudeis hekōn ponēros, oud' akōn makar]. He understands by ponēros, with the accent on the last syllable, a wicked man; whereas without doubt the old saying intends ponēros with the accent on the first, that is, a man full of toil and hardship. For "toilworn" [poneros] and "blessed" [makar] are rightly opposed; "wicked" [ponēros] and "blessed" [ p. 363 ] not at all. The same is the case, and the same too the signification, of the word mochthēros, which means a wicked man, and mochtheros, which means one who leads a life of hardship; both words yield the superlative mochthērotatos; whence that question founded on the ambiguity, proposed to Stratonicus in the eighth book of Athenaeus' Deipnosophists: "Who are the mochthērotatoi [most wretched, or most wicked] of the people in Pamphylia?" since the life of the Pamphylians was both miserable and infamous for brigandage. Nay, that the greatest men have sometimes been deceived as to the sense of words where an ambiguous word occurs with no distinguishing accent, although an attentive consideration of the whole context [Greek: periochē] seems precisely to demand one particular sense, let Marcus Tullius himself be the example, the most learned man in Greek and Latin since men were born. He, in the first book of the Tusculans, cap. xlii., reports thus the words of Leonidas to the Spartans who were about to fight against Xerxes in such a manner that they believed they would without doubt die in the battle: "Go on with brave spirit; perhaps we shall sup among the dead below." Now ariston is "breakfast"; it also signifies the best or bravest in war, derived from Ares. But Diodorus Siculus, lib. xi. cap. ix., says that Leonidas ordered them quickly to take their breakfast; he said, "Breakfast! or, Make your breakfast quickly!" [Greek: Aristate! Aristopoieite tacheōs!], that is, "Eat your morning meal in haste." This sense the logic of the antithesis demands; namely, "since we are to sup among the dead." But Cicero, not attending to the opposition, and taking aristan as if it were said [like aristeuein, "to be bravest"] from aristos, which signifies a brave man, translated it, "Go on with brave spirit." Other instances [Greek: enstaseis] are at hand, but the ill-founded comparison will not permit me to dwell longer on them. You will say, perhaps, that the punctation also rests upon authority to this extent, that it is utterly impious to cast it away. But it has all its authority from its authors, and from nowhere else. That we may come, therefore, to the point at which we are aiming, we shall consider in few words who, according to the opinion of those who assail the divine origin of the punctation, were its authors.
XI. Those who deny that the Bible was pointed by Ezra, as we said before, assign that work with one voice to certain Masoretes or Tiberian Jewish rabbis. In designating the time they hold contrary opinions. They conjecture that certain Masoretes lived at Tiberias after the completion of both Talmudic works, which they make prior to this invention [Greek: heurēma] of the points; conjecture, I say, for they cannot prove it by any adequate argument or testimony. That the Jews had a school in that place some centuries earlier, we grant; but we say it was deserted and desolate by the time the Babylonian Talmud was compiled. But let us grant, since learned men are so very eager for it, that some Jewish rabbis, more or fewer, lived at that time at Tiberias; let us look into who and of what sort they were, and what is to be ascribed to their learning, judgment, and integrity, detracting nothing injuriously from their reputation.
XII. I do not wish here to repeat what I have written elsewhere on the state, condition, and horrible hardening of the Jews after the second temple and the final rejection of the nation by God. Let us satisfy the present design by subjoining things which, being known to all, can be denied by none. That the greatest privilege of the Jews of old was "that the oracles of God were entrusted to them" [Greek: hoti episteuthēsan ta logia tou Theou], [ p. 364 ] the apostle relates, Rom. 3:2. But from that privilege they had long since fallen; for the Word is no longer in any degree committed to their care or trust. For the word had been entrusted to their fathers by divine covenant, Isa. 59:21; and when they had fallen from that covenant through unbelief, and had ceased to be the people and church of God, they began to be possessors of the word itself only in bad faith. With the legitimate right to the use of the word, they also utterly lost the Holy Spirit, promised, namely, in the same covenant. What ought to be expected in or about the word of God from men to whom it is in no way entrusted, and who are destitute of the Spirit, it would be easy to show, were they not themselves a horrendous example of it. Approaching the most holy word of God with unwashed hands, impure in conscience and in mind, themselves contaminated by everyone, they foully contaminate all sacred things; for "to the defiled and unbelieving nothing is pure, but even their mind and conscience are defiled" [Greek: memiasmenois kai apistois ouden katharon, alla memiantai autōn kai ho nous kai hē syneidēsis], as the apostle speaks of these very men, Titus 1:15. Then, so far were they from perceiving the true sense of the Holy Spirit in the word, that they set themselves obstinately, stubbornly, and most pertinaciously against the whole truth which is solely contained in those books which they daily wore out with their hands. Whatever in the whole Scripture pertains to the glory of God, whatever to their own salvation, all this was to them an object of the greatest hatred and abomination. Nay, from the time of Rabbi Judah the Prince, who composed the Mishnah, and onwards, they most superstitiously cultivated a certain new religion, plainly alien from that prescribed to the fathers, that is, from the Mosaic theology, and diametrically opposed to the Christian religion, which that older one served as handmaid. For, pressed continually by the horrible curse of God for having repudiated and slain his most holy Son, the Savior of the world, they have striven in vain to extricate themselves from its bond and penalty by new crimes; stupid meanwhile, and ignorant beyond all belief of all the good arts and sciences, of all history, and of all things useful in human life. That their greatest masters were a race of men inept, futile, raving, magical, idolatrous, superstitious, unlearned, and depraved, both Talmuds teach; and so occupied in devising portentous fables, that they seem to have descended together into the arena to outdo one another in a contest of lying. Whoever can persuade himself that the biblical punctation proceeded from manikins of this sort, most shameless, vain, foolish, lying, accursed, and hateful to God, and that it was forthwith received and employed by all Jews and Christians, without any previous examination or any hesitation whatever, as the norm of all reading, both private and public, and even of all expositions and interpretations, shall keep his credulity to himself without me for a rival.
XIII. I know indeed that very learned men have used other and more numerous arguments in this cause; let it suffice me to have briefly indicated those which with me are of such weight that I cannot, and would not, depart from that opinion which is fortified by their authority. The learned men also [ p. 365 ] who have undertaken the patronage of the rabbinical origin of the punctation have reasons which they put forward for their opinion, and these remain to be considered.
XIV. In the first place they recount the names of certain great men with whom the opinion that asserts the novelty of the points has the preference. Yet a hundred years ago, or thereabouts, it would have been impossible for anyone to use this argument. It is scarcely a century since this audacious conjecture became widespread; nor has it so much stability but that there is hope it may vanish in a lesser space of time. And although I am a man than whom none rises more willingly at the names of learned men, yet there is more than one reason why I should not sweat much in this place over an argument taken from the testimony of certain persons. For since there is no lack of names not less great, of celebrated men of no slighter fame in the republic of letters, whom it would be the easiest thing to set against these, the prudent reader will judge that no decision of this dispute can follow from a conflict of this kind, not so much of testimonies as of men. Moreover, I find that learned men do not always use that diligence which is fitting in citing authors. Let those serve as an example who cite Valentinus Schindler with approval, as if he supported the opinion of the rabbinical invention of the points; whereas under the word shabal he shows that he judged the points to be more ancient even than the age of Ezra. Some are in doubt; others settle this dispute in passing and while engaged on other business; those who have undertaken the defense of that opinion of set purpose are exceedingly few. And as for some of these, who by their merit were of great name in theology and in the church of God while they lived, and still are among a not ungrateful posterity, I judge that if they could have foreseen to what end certain men would use their words, they would have pronounced nothing in this cause except after an accurate weighing of the moments of the reasons.
XV. Secondly, they say: "The ancient Hebrew letters were the Samaritan; but the Samaritans never had vowel points; and therefore neither did the ancient Hebrews." I answer: If learned men were not at a loss for arguments to confirm their opinion, they would doubtless not lean upon most uncertain conjectures of this sort. That the ancient Hebrew letters were those very ones which are still in use among the Jews, we have proved above. It is also plainly uncertain whether the Samaritans of old used vowel points in writing. Postellus affirms that they did. Nor is there any reason why anyone should wonder that that stupid people lost the use and skill of so excellent an artifice. Furthermore: suppose that Ezra repudiated the ancient letters for new ones, either devised by himself or borrowed from elsewhere; by what reasoning, pray, would it seem to follow from this that he who introduced those new letters did not also invent the points? But since this argument has at last been cast aside as feeble and useless by the very men who formerly used it, there is no cause why I should pursue it further.
XVI. Thirdly, they say: "The Jews keep in their synagogues a book of the law written without [ p. 366 ] points, which they all but adore, and which they affirm represents the autograph of Moses; that Israelites are found even in China, in whose most ancient books no points appear, although some of them were written six hundred years ago; from which, they say, it is evident that not Moses or Ezra, but the post-Talmudic rabbis devised the artifice of the Hebrew punctation." I answer: How light this argument is, nay, of how little weight in this cause, we have shown elsewhere. Here we shall only run through the heads of the answers. We say therefore, first, that this practice of the Jews is proved by no adequate testimony to be universal, that is, common to the whole nation in all synagogues everywhere; and it matters nothing to us what a few western Jews do in their synagogues, since the practice of the greatest number is wholly unknown to us. Secondly, no one has yet shown that it is even ancient; most of those synagogue customs are superstitious and idolatrous, very many are newfangled, and all post-Talmudic. Thirdly, the Jews themselves, at least the most numerous and most learned of them, constantly deny that they intend by that practice to teach that the ancient Bibles were unpointed, since they themselves adhere to the opinion of the divine origin of the whole punctation. Fourthly, that synagogue book, although it has no points, nevertheless has the Keri and Kethib, which those who use this argument will concede to be no more from Moses than the points themselves. Either, then, the codex, or rather the custom of certain Jews, does not prove that the points are not coeval with the letters, or it proves that the Keri and Kethib are so coeval with the text. Fifthly, those who most audaciously seize this synagogue practice of the Jews for their own purpose say that the rabbis devised the points for a twofold reason: first, namely, that the reading of the sacred text might be rendered more expeditious, and as easy for others as for themselves; then, that some who were less learned might by their help read the biblical text in the synagogues; they themselves therefore concede that pointed codices were formerly accustomed to be read in the synagogues. Nay, it is most certain that in antiquity the Hellenizing Jews used the Greek version of the law in their synagogues; so that nothing certain can be gathered from this modern custom of some. Sixthly, the Jews themselves render other reasons for this custom; some of which we have elsewhere recounted from Azariah's Imre Binah, cap. lix. And whoever weighs those twenty-one rules which the masters lay down as necessarily to be observed in copying out a codex that is to be employed in the sacred services of the synagogue will easily perceive that it is not without cause that they employ an unpointed codex, since the accurate observation of all those rules in the writing of the points is plainly impossible. But the chief, if not the only, reason of this custom seems to consist in this: namely, that no one should presume to read the law publicly in the synagogues until he be so far skilled that he can exactly observe all the points, both vowel and accentual, in the reading of codices in which they are not written. For those who recite that book not only distinguish all the vowel points most diligently in pronouncing, but also chant every single word to the modulation of the accents. Another reason also for this custom, [ p. 367 ] perhaps the truest of all, which we too have indicated elsewhere, can be gathered from what Johannes Morinus most truly pronounces, Grammatica Samaritana, cap. xxv. "Writing of this kind," he says, "is liable to far more senses than that which writes out the words with their vowels, and is therefore far more fertile in mystical interpretations, which can be learned only from tradition handed down from the fathers [Greek: patroparadotos]." Finally: suppose they say that by this book they mean to represent the autograph [Greek: autographon] of Moses, which some of them feign to have been written like one single word, without any distinction of words; yet that Ezra did not point the Bible in no way follows from this; and therefore this practice of the Jews contributes nothing at all to the settling of this dispute.
XVII. Fourthly, they adduce the testimony of Elias Levita, who lived in Germany about the year 1520. He not only openly affirms that the post-Talmudic Tiberian Masoretes invented the points, but labors to prove it by several arguments. And what then, pray? "Therefore that opinion is most true." How so? For what cause ought the one Elias to be of greater authority with us than very many others, far earlier in age, no less celebrated in learning? It would be easy to overwhelm his testimony not only by the number but by the weight of the others. But, they say, there is no lack of reason why the authority of the one Elias ought to be preferred to that of all the other masters of the same profession who think the contrary. And what reason is that? The Jews, they say, always itch to ascribe to themselves and their own everything that in any degree redounds to the honor of the nation. To that end they lie most impudently everywhere. No faith, therefore, is to be placed in them when speaking in their own cause. But since Elias embraces an opinion which detracts much from the antiquity, and so from the honor, of the points, he must be supposed to have gone over to it only under compulsion of the force of truth. But the matter stands far otherwise. Since the rabbinical name was born, nothing has ever been done, either by these men or by the whole nation, that can be compared with this illustrious invention. What, pray, could be feigned more magnificent for the honor of the nation than that some of them were furnished with such wisdom and skill in the sense of the Scriptures that they could, and with such sanctity and fidelity that they would, invent and bring forth the biblical punctation, by the consent of all the most consummate and most useful work that has ever come to light since men were born? I would dare to say that all the adversaries of the divine origin of the points would most willingly concede that the whole Jewish nation, from the day on which they were rejected by God, has never undertaken or perfected anything that is to be set beside the invention of the points. I would not deny that some of the masters, after they applied themselves to the Peripatetic philosophy recovered by the Saracens into the light and use of men, ceased to play the fool, and wrote things which may be read with some profit. But that all the more ancient ones, Mishnic and Talmudic alike, patched together nothing but ravings, dreams, impostures, and lies, all the learned agree, and the thing itself bears witness. That the punctation [ p. 368 ] was affixed to the sacred letters with the highest judgment, conscience, love of truth, and knowledge, there is no one, that I know of, who denies. There is nothing, therefore, that would redound equally to the honor of the whole nation and of the Tiberian masters, nothing that would equally consign all Christians into bondage to the rabbis, as that Elian figment of the rabbinical invention of the punctation. And indeed it is very probable that Elias was carried into that opinion for no other cause than that he well understood that, this once admitted and established, all students of the sacred letters must necessarily acknowledge themselves bound by an immortal benefit to those Tiberian rabbis.
XVIII. They add, moreover, the testimony of Aben Ezra, whose antiquity and authority are greater. He, in his commentary on Exod. 25 [31], has these words: "I have seen in the books which the wise men of Tiberias examined, concerning which some of their elders would swear that they three times diligently considered every word, every punctation, and every term, full or defective; and behold, a yod was written in the word te'aseh ['it shall be made']; but I did not find it so in the books of Spain and France, nor in those beyond the sea." Readers will easily perceive that they labor under a great penury of testimonies who produce ones of this kind, which are so far from bringing succor to the opinion they would have asserted that they plainly confirm the contrary. They say, I confess, "that the Tiberians examined those codices in order to know whether the punctation answered exactly to the true pronunciation." But nothing of the kind occurs in the author himself. He affirms only that they examined those codices in order to know, namely, whether they were exactly written. Nor was the question he agitates about any point, but about the letter yod, which he teaches ought to be in the word te'aseh, as appears from the accurate examination of those codices. He says, therefore, that some of the writers of those codices (that the authority of those which he himself had seen, in which the yod is found written in that word, might be the more clearly established) swore that they had diligently examined every term, every word, and the whole punctation; according, namely, to the norm of those ancient exemplars which they had in their keeping. Nor does it any more follow from this that they devised the points than the letters themselves, since they subjected to examination every term and every word, that is, all the letters, no less than the punctation. Rabbi Aben Ezra, therefore, relates nothing else of those Tiberian elders than that they swore they had most diligently examined the writing of those codices (one of which he himself had seen), consisting of letters and points.
XIX. Fifthly, some urge the silence of both Talmuds about the points; not so the renowned Vossius, who contends that no mention of them is made in the Mishnah, but confesses that mention is made in both Talmuds. Two things, however, are presupposed in this argument: first, that both Talmuds are utterly silent about the points, for if they are mentioned in any one place anywhere, the whole force of this testimony will instantly vanish; and then, that from the silence of the Talmuds it necessarily follows that the use of the points was at that time unknown.
## [ p. 369 ]

XX. First, then, learned men deny that any mention of the points is made in the whole Talmudic work. But since it is all too probable that some of those who use this argument have by no means read through those huge volumes so diligently that they can safely pronounce that the whole of them is utterly silent about the points, they point out certain places in which, if the points had then been known or in use, it could not have happened, themselves being judges, that they should fail to be mentioned. I answer: For my part I would by no means concede that the points had not yet been devised, even if it were established beyond contradiction [Greek: anantirrhētōs] that no mention of them is made in the Talmudic work, since it is most certain that there are very many things pertaining to the doctrine of the Hebrew language about which the deepest silence reigns in it; so that I could pass over this whole argument without the least loss or prejudice to the truth. Nevertheless, since there is no lack of testimonies drawn from both Talmuds which prove that the punctation was known at the time when those works were published, it is clearer than light itself that this argument is exceedingly feeble and plainly of no strength. There is no need for me to transfer hither the places which the most erudite Buxtorf produced in his Tiberias, and which his most learned son solidly vindicated from the exceptions of Cappellus. Let the reader consult what Rabbi Azariah has collected in Imre Binah, 59, from the Jerusalem Talmud, Megillah, cap. iv., and the Babylonian Talmud, Megillah i., fol. 3, and Nedarim, fol. 37.
XXI. But they urge further that no mention is made of the points precisely where it would evidently have been necessary, if only the artifice of pointing had then been devised; for when the Talmudic doctors wished to distinguish the various senses of the words dabar and also banayikh ["your sons" or "your builders"], which signify plainly different things according to the diversity of the vowels which may be affixed to the letters or pronounced with them, they say nothing at all about vowel points, but by repeating the words themselves over again they show that they signified by pronunciation a distinction which they could not mark in writing. For how easy would it have been for them to indicate that this word, written or pronounced with kamets, or with tsere, or with holem, or hirik, signifies this or that, if only those points had been known to them? I answer: We concede that the vowel points were not of old distinguished by technical names. These were imposed upon them at the discretion of the grammarians long afterwards; so it has been done in other languages also. The Masoretes very often call tsere simply kamets, and sometimes kamets katon, those names being not yet distinguished. Segol likewise is called by them patach katon, and kibbuts, shurek. The open sounds which they denote were their first names. Although, therefore, the Talmudic doctors could perhaps not designate the vowels by those names by which the later age of the grammarians distinguished them from one another, yet nothing hinders us from reckoning that they knew how to write their figures. Next, how can it be proved that the Talmud was written without points? The Jews constantly assert that the Mishnah, more ancient than either Talmud, was anciently pointed, although for many centuries those codices have nowhere appeared; [ p. 370 ] until very recently it was again carefully edited at Amsterdam. Now the whole force of this argument hangs on the assumption that they did not use points in writing at that time; which is the very point at issue [Greek: en archē, the thing assumed at the start]. Furthermore, the renowned Buxtorf showed long ago that in those places where the various sense of those little words is treated, the train of the discourse is such that there is no need of the addition of points to assign the distinction.
XXII. Sixthly, Cappellus draws an argument against the antiquity of the punctation from the number of the vowels and accents, which is said to be laborious and useless. The vowels are fourteen; many of them, or at least some, are said to be useless, since there are not so many distinct sounds in human speech. Then they say that no account can be given of several of the accents, since they signify nothing at all, distinguish nothing, exhibit no meaning, and are of no help or assistance in learning the language or in reading. The points of both kinds, therefore, vowel and accentual alike, seem to have been a rabbinical invention [Greek: heurēma], and one not even well fitted to the very purpose it serves. But there are not wanting others who think they can reach the same end from a consideration of the number and distinction of the vowels by a means plainly contrary. For, they say, those points exhibit all the open sounds or vowels so exactly and accurately that they appear an invention too artfully devised to be ascribed to the first inventors of letters, whose care was to express only what was necessary. Thus in the judgment of some they are superfluous and useless; of others, accurately fitted to the vowel sounds; of neither, for reasons plainly contrary, divine. But the solution of both these petty objections is easy. Those who acknowledge a most accurate perfection in this punctation have without doubt no just cause for removing it from the divine order. The more perfect anything is in its kind, the more divine it ought to be reckoned. And the most learned men judge, not without the weightiest reasons, that the Hebrew language, the most ancient and most perfect of all languages, was put forth as the norm of the others by God himself and by divine men furnished with the prophetic spirit. Nor would I deny that there is force in this reasoning, did we not assign the origin of the points to a most perfect wisdom, comprehending all things at once. But the discourse of those who plead that several of the vowels and accents are useless is plainly futile, and proud. How, pray, will those petty doctors ever make us believe that everything in the sacred letters whose reason they do not grasp is useless? Is this to deal seriously? Is this to argue? "We do not understand the reason, use, and utility of many of the accents; therefore they are superfluous, and were devised by the rabbis at their discretion." It would indeed be easy to settle any dispute whatever in that fashion. But ought we to make determinations about the word of God from our own ignorance and want of knowledge? Will that, in the end, be reckoned fair? For my part I would rather give my faith to the man who professed to know nothing than to him who professed to know everything. What if some one man, utterly unknown to us, has been helped by some accent in perceiving the sense of even the most minute particle [ p. 371 ] in the sacred letters, or directed in reading in that manner which God approved while the carnal institutions of the Jewish church flourished; shall we manikins, to whom, among innumerable other things, that use is unknown, judge it useless? Beyond doubt, had not learned men determined beforehand with themselves, for other reasons, that the punctation was not of divine origin, they would not have endured to use that audacious cavil in this cause. "The Jews indeed think that no one exists today to whom the complete rationale of the accents is known," writes Sebastian Munster, in the epistle prefixed to the commentary of Rabbi David Kimchi on Amos. Elias Levita, indeed, in a Hebrew epistle to the same Munster, testifies that he wrote a book on the rationale of the accents, which perished at Rome. If that writing had been published, perhaps learned and modest men would possess something of whose knowledge they would by no means repent.
XXIII. Seventhly, they contend that Jerome also knew nothing of the points, since, as they say, he nowhere made any mention of them. They suppose, it seems, that that most learned man committed to writing everything he knew, whether he had any occasion of doing so or not; in which opinion I cannot agree with them. Next, I would have them show what reason Jerome had for relating that the Hebrews in writing their language used not only letters but vowels also, when all the other nations known to him did the same. There is no ancient historiographer who has handed down to memory more, and more certain, things about the affairs of the Carthaginians than Polybius of Megalopolis. He shows everywhere not only that he understood the Punic language, but also that he had read their records. For when in the third book of his history he had accurately taught with what forces Hannibal entered Italy, he affirms that he gathered all that he related from bronze tablets inscribed by Hannibal himself. And yet this Polybius nowhere in his histories mentions the unusual manner of writing from right to left which the Carthaginians used, of which nevertheless he himself could not have been ignorant. Little help, therefore, is to be expected for this opinion from the silence of Jerome. For it is certain that he could have known the points and not mentioned them in his writings; it is certain that they could have existed and yet escaped him, since many copies were always written out without them; it is certain also that Jerome obtained the use of very few copies, and employed but one or two masters; so that it ought to seem by no means strange if he had never seen a pointed codex; and there were no grammars, nor had anyone at that time cultivated the technical art of that language. To lean on his silence, therefore, is to contend against the truth not with arguments or judgments, but with most uncertain conjectures. But neither are testimonies wanting, drawn from the works of Jerome, which openly indicate that he had both seen and known the points of both kinds. Elsewhere I have cited that place in the epistle to Evagrius: "The Hebrews very rarely use the vowel letters in the middle." The same words are cited in the same sense by Rabbi Azariah in Imre Binah, cap. lix., [ p. 372 ] by Simeon de Muis, Defensio Veritatis Hebraicae, and by Joseph de Voysin, preface to the Pugio Fidei. They answer that Jerome called the letters aleph, vav, yod vowel letters, since in other languages they perform that office. True, yod and vav are vowels, namely when they have points adjoined; although very often they take the place of consonants, otherwise than happens in the Greek language. That aleph was ever a vowel is not yet proved; it is also frequently pronounced with a sound different from that which it would pour forth if it were a vowel. Those bare letters, therefore, are not the vowels Jerome indicates. Then, in his exposition of the third chapter of Jonah he has these words: "I wonder much why it was so translated, since in the Hebrew there is no community either of letters, or of syllables, or of accents, or of the word." I know not whether anyone could make more open mention of the accents; and no one will say that these were in use before the vowel points. So on Gen. 19:33, at the word be-qumah ["when she arose"], "They point it above," he says, "as though it were incredible that anyone should lie with a woman without knowing it," observing the irregular punctation [the extraordinary point over the word]. And on Ezek. 27: "Hebrew nouns are frequently interpreted variously according to the diversity of the accents and the change of the letters and vowels, which among them most especially have their own proprieties." And in the epistle to Paula and Eustochium, or the preface to Isaiah, he affirms that that prophecy "is written among the Hebrews in cola and commata." In the Hebrew Questions on Gen. 47:31: "And in this place," he says, "some feign to no purpose that Jacob adored the top of Joseph's scepter, namely that in honoring his son he adored his power; whereas in the Hebrew it reads far otherwise: And Israel adored toward the head of the bed." The word about which the question arises is mittah ["bed"]. Jerome knew that the apostle had rendered that word by "staff" [Greek: rhabdos] (Heb. 11:21). If it had not been so pointed that it ought really to be rendered "bed," how, pray, would he have dared to say "in the Hebrew it reads otherwise," when without points it does not read otherwise? Ought Jerome to have hearkened to his Nicodemus [his Jewish instructor] against the translation employed by the apostle himself, or the sense of the place rendered by him, and boldly pronounce "in the Hebrew it reads otherwise"? which, unless we concede that he knew the points, is most false. It is therefore sufficiently established that Jerome knew the vowel points and the accents, and made more open mention of them than was to be expected from a man who had no other occasions of recording them. But some say that Jerome could have learned from common usage that the word mittah was to be pronounced in that place in such a way as to signify "bed." But in Jerome there is nothing about the pronunciation of the word; he shows how it was read in the Hebrew, that is, by every reader inspecting the text; and what, pray, was that common usage which he is feigned to have followed? He employed, as we said, one or two masters. But that he preferred the whispering of an obscure master to the authority of the apostle is not credible; especially since the train of the discourse admits both senses; for it is most certain that Jacob adored God leaning on the top of his staff.
XXIV. In the last place, some plead the kinship of the Arabic and Syriac languages with the Hebrew; [ p. 373 ] and these, they say, admitted punctation only very recently, and the condition of the Hebrew language in antiquity seems to have been the same as theirs. I answer: At what time the Arabs and Syrians first obtained the use of letters, and at what time the use of points, is plainly uncertain. That the ancient Arabic character fell out of use centuries ago is shown by the renowned Golius, preface to Surah 31 of the Koran. What other changes the vicissitudes of times and of all things have introduced into that language we plainly do not know. Perhaps the points also passed into desuetude, and were recalled into common use by the Saracens. In truth, as to when the vowel points first began to be in use among the Arabs and Syrians, there exists no testimony worthy of credit. Suppose, too, that all those oriental languages lacked vowel points from the beginning; what hinders Ezra from being reckoned to have added them in the use of the sacred language?
XXV. And these are the chief heads of the arguments by which learned men think they have proved that the Hebrew punctation was an invention [Greek: heurēma] of the post-Talmudic rabbis; and those who suffer themselves to be cast out of the possession of so great a treasure by such arguments shall by no means have me for a companion.
XXVI. Many also judge that from that care which Ezra applied to the sacred Scriptures proceeded that well-known variation in the sacred Bible which goes under the name of Keri and Kethib. It does not please me to repeat here what we have written elsewhere on the nature and number of those variant readings. There are those who ascribe this variety to the first amanuenses of the Holy Spirit; of these, some think a mystery lies beneath it; others, that the ambiguity of the bath kol [Hebrew: bat qol, the "daughter of a voice"] was its cause. There are those who assert that the Kerioth are Masoretic notes, partly various readings collected out of divers codices, partly critical corrections of the text; this is the opinion of men who care little what is thought of the purity of the Hebrew codex, which they call Masoretic, as if there were need here of a distinguishing label, or as if any other biblical codex had ever been in the use of men. Having read through the opinions and arguments of many learned men, I confess that here my stream runs aground; yet I am glad to add what Isaac Abarbanel has on this matter, in his preface to the prophet Jeremiah. Having rejected the less convenient opinion of others, he adds: "But the matter seems to me truly to stand thus: Ezra, with the men of the great synagogue, found the books perfect and entire, just as they had been written; but before he instituted the punctation and the accents and the distinction of the verses, he attended to the reading; and if anything occurred to him alien from the nature or propriety of the language, and from the rules of grammar, he reckoned with himself that it had come about for one or the other of two causes. First, that the scribe in these irregular forms had regard to some one of the mysteries of the law, according to the dignity of his prophecy and the profundity of his wisdom, and therefore he did not dare to attempt to delete anything from the divine books; for he understood that those books had been written with singular wisdom, and that the defective and redundant letters [ p. 374 ] and outlandish expressions of that kind had not been written without cause; wherefore he left them in the text just as they had really been written, but in the margin he placed the Keri, or the exposition of this exotic writing according to the propriety of the language and the simple sense... It may also be that Ezra thought that there existed in the sacred Scripture letters and expressions irregularly written for no other cause than that he who uttered them had not expressed them correctly, as was proper, whether through want of skill in the Hebrew language or through want of skill in orthography; ... and therefore it was needful that the true writing and reading of the expression according to the rules of grammar should be set forth. This is the rationale of the Keri, which the holy scribe placed in the margin, fearing to lay hands on the words of authors speaking by the inspiration of the Holy Spirit."
# Chapter XIII

Chapter argument: The remaining parts of the Ezraitic reformation of the church; the restoration of the solemn preaching of the divine word, Neh. 8:1, 8; its neglect before the captivity; Jehoshaphat's endeavor for its restoration, 2 Chron. 17:7-9; princes joined to the priests and Levites in that work; the diligence and success of the first reformers of the Christian churches in the same; the trifles of the Jews, and of others; the excommunication of the Cuthaeans, Ezra 4:2, 3; the separation of the people from impure mixtures.
I. We proceed in the reformation of the church returned from captivity. In the third place we assigned to these reformers their supreme diligence in the preaching of the divine word, by which the returning people, who had long lain in the darkness of Babylon, might be instructed in the knowledge of God and in the right performance of their duty. There stands the history of this truly divine undertaking, Neh. 8:1, 8: "All the people gathered themselves together;... for they read in that book, in the law of God, distinctly, and by expounding gave the sense, and caused them to understand through that scripture." That duty of instructing the people by sacred discourses and solemn expositions of the word of God had been despised and neglected during the former defection, so far, namely, as it pertained to the ordinary care of the priests and Levites. The extraordinary preaching of the word by the prophets was so much a matter of divine administration that on that account the church was neither to be praised nor reproached. God had ordained that "the lips of the priests should preserve knowledge," Mal. 2:7. From them it was the duty of the people to seek the law of God, that is, the sense of the mind of God set forth in the law. The priests, accordingly, God severely rebukes for the neglect of this work, Hos. 4:6. On the Levites also lay the charge of instructing the people, Deut. 33:10. To Jehoshaphat, therefore, when he undertook to reform the church according to the will of God, nothing was more important than the care of the preaching of that word through the whole church; and since the priests and Levites were unequal to the charge, whether through fewness of number, or because very many of them were themselves entangled in the crime of a foul ignorance of the divine law, by common right he joined to them other men, princes and magnates, who had the reformation of the church at heart, [ p. 375 ] 2 Chron. 17:7-9. For the neglect of the administration of this charge is a most certain proof [Greek: tekmērion] of ecclesiastical defection; and exactness in it, not the least part of reformation. By the godly reformers, therefore, account was taken of this duty with all possible diligence. To that work also did those men apply themselves, not without admirable success, whom it pleased God to use in recovering his churches from the long servitude of mystical Babylon.
II. The more recent Jews think that the Chaldee paraphrase is indicated by those words which we have brought forward out of Nehemiah. Some Christians also either accede to that opinion or show themselves akin to that delusion, having first determined to be enslaved to a hypothesis [Greek: douleuein hypothesei] in another opinion no less absurd. But we have shown above that the use and skill of the Hebrew language had not yet perished from the people, as they would have it. Furthermore, it is expressly said that these Ezraitic preachers first read the book of the law "distinctly," and then by "exposition of the sense gave understanding." To what end, pray, would that distinct reading have served, if the common people had not at all understood the very sound of the words? What is recorded, therefore, was a solemn restitution of the divine institution of the assiduous preaching of the word. There follows the excommunication of the Cuthaeans, or rather their rejection. The history stands at Ezra 4:2, 3: "They came to Zerubbabel, and to the chief of the people, and said to them, Let us build with you: for like you we will seek your God; for we have not sacrificed to another since the days of Esarhaddon king of Assyria, who brought us up hither. But Zerubbabel said to them, and Jeshua, with the rest of the chiefs of the paternal families of Israel, It is not for you to build with us a house to our God; but we who are together will build to Jehovah the God of Israel." It was not participation in divine worship, but the favor of the Persian kings, which the Jews then enjoyed, that these hypocrites courted. For if they had sought with a sincere mind to join themselves to the God of Israel, they were not to be rejected. But it was plainly another matter, as appeared from the event; nor, while pretending religion, had they anything else in their minds than what, so many ages before, the Shechemites had in a like case, Gen. 34:23.
III. To the rejection of the Samaritans was added the separation from impure mixtures with profane peoples. That is related at large in the ninth and tenth chapters of Ezra, and in the last chapter of Nehemiah. And thus at length was completed that celebrated [Greek: diaboētos] reformation of the church, and the restitution of the Mosaic theology to its pristine place, honor, use, and dignity.
# Chapter XIV

Chapter argument: The final apostasy of the Jewish church; its general occasions; the people mingled with the Gentiles; the entrance of philosophy; the various evils sprung from it; disputations, quarrels; sects; the forgetting of the holy tongue; the oppression and ignorance of the common people; the ambition and blindness of the doctors; the corruption of morals; versions of the Scriptures.

I. The grace of God in the restoration of the church was followed, in process of time, by the defection of ungrateful men, and that by a horrible fall and ruin. [ p. 376 ] The plan of our undertaking requires that we also set this forth in few words. The new church, founded by the prophetic ministry, retained while that ministry lasted, though not free from all fault, a purity pleasing to the merciful God. When that was taken away, the whole fabric of the church gradually inclined toward ruin. We shall briefly point out the rough places that drove it into straits from which it could never emerge, and then the nature of its total defection.
II. From the time the reformation was completed, the people, crushed by the tyranny and oppressions of the kings of Syria and Egypt, dispersed hither and thither on every side by various calamities, and compelled even to bear foreigners in the holy land, was straightway infected with the contagion of very many evils. In those ages the Greeks brought forth into the light, far and wide through the whole east, that eristic philosophy packed with empty wranglings, which they had not long before been laboring to bring to birth, the arms of the Macedonians playing the midwife. This the doctors of the Jews also drank in. While the temple of Solomon stood, and they worshiped God in it chastely, with sincere and due worship, that philosophy was utterly unknown to all the godly. For before the Babylonian captivity no mention occurs among that nation of polite letters or of exotic sciences, with the single exception of the mathematics, which, received from the Chaldeans, they seem to have perverted to idolatrous uses. For all human wisdom is prone to pride and superstition [Greek: deisidaimonia], especially where it sits in a mind not subdued to the obedience of the truth. But that Greekish philosophy, gradually introduced into the church, very swiftly issued not only in a great evil, but in the destruction of the purer theology. For hence those whose religion consisted in faith alone and the observance of the commandments of God began to erect schools wholly unlike those over which the ancient prophets presided, to institute endless disputations, to tie knots, and to fly apart into sects, not otherwise than we know happened among the Greeks by reason of that philosophy. The names of Plato, of Aristotle, of Zeno were not more the badges of disputatious mobs among the Greeks than those of Shammai, Hillel, and others among the Jews. The Pharisees, Sadducees, and Essenes rivaled the Academics, the Peripatetics, and the Stoics; but the itch for disputing in the former proved more pernicious than in the latter, because they entered into quarrels not about goat's wool, that is, about figments which like spiders they wove out of their own brains, as those Greeks did, but about religion, about the worship of God, about the sense of the Scriptures, all of them things sacred and to be adored.
III. Next, the people had in great part forgotten the holy tongue. This was the second fruit of the mixture with the Gentiles. Those who lived in the dispersions spoke Greek; those in their ancestral seats, Syriac. Thus the common people was little by little brought into the deepest ignorance of the law of God, since it scarcely understood the language in which alone it was written. Relying on that ignorance, some petty doctors did not hesitate, casting away all zeal for the fear of God, to raise their crests, to arrogate everything to themselves as if they alone were wise, to despise the people and lord it over their faith, to assume magisterial titles, and to contend with one another. [ p. 377 ] It would be far too long to pursue in this place the origins, distinctions, opinions, crimes, and pretexts of the sects. Since for the plan of the work I must move among things well known and already occupied by others, it pleases me only to touch briefly their heads. Let it suffice, then, to have summarily numbered among the causes of the general defection the ignorance of the common people and the pride, vanity, and blindness of the doctors, since the former were destitute of the very letter, the latter of the Spirit.
IV. Add to this that, after the Hasmoneans had for some time gained the mastery of affairs, the theocracy [Greek: theokratia] divinely instituted being cast aside, they openly defected in political government to Greekish customs and rites. Greek were the names, Hyrcanus, Alexander, Aristobulus, Antigonus, of those who ruled the people after the Macedonian fashion. And from that source also arose no small evils. Since, therefore, the prophetic ministry had ceased in the church, while the people, exposed to profane mixtures with the Gentiles, had almost forgotten its own tongue, and the doctors, flying apart into various sects after the manner of the philosophers, consulted only their own honor, and there was no one to cut down the crops of enormities growing up daily, everything was openly hastening to final ruin. But before I recount the heads of this fatal defection, it is necessary that we say something of the versions of sacred Scripture, which very many judge to have had their origin about these times, when the people had lost its skill in the holy tongue; and there is hope that these matters can be dispatched in the briefest of digressions.
# Digression II. On the Greek Version of the Sacred Scriptures by the Seventy Elders

I. Concerning the Greek version of the Scriptures by the Seventy, which is commonly assigned to this interval of time, while the Jewish church was on the slope toward its general defection, I find it necessary to say what Protagoras of old, as Laertius reports, lib. ix. 51, said of the gods of the nations. "Concerning the gods," said he, "I am not able to know either that they are or that they are not; for many things hinder knowing: the obscurity of the matter, and the shortness of man's life" [Greek: Peri men theōn ouk echō eidenai, eith' hōs eisin, eith' hōs ouk eisin...]. For everything that is commonly said about that version is so uncertain, and so many and so great are the sword-fights of opinions about it, that the span of a life would scarcely suffice for the accurate examination of them all; and so we have seen what he once said come true: many men, themselves uncertain, contending over this matter.
II. Very many agree that some version of the Scriptures was made in these times by seventy-two men; yet there are not wanting those who repudiate the whole history of it as damaged goods and a merely Jewish fable. Irenaeus and Clement of Alexandria assign that work to the time of Ptolemy the son of Lagus; Aristeas, the well-known father of the history, as found in Josephus, and Josephus himself, to that of Philadelphus; some, to that of Philometor. Justin, in his second Apology, is authority for the statement that Philadelphus sent to Herod, king of the Jews, asking him to send those interpreters to him. Such a lapse in history [Greek: anistorēsia], or chronological oversight [Greek: parorama chronikon], in so eminent a man [ p. 378 ] proves that he lent too easy a faith to the rumors which were commonly tossed about concerning that version. Most write that Demetrius Phalereus was the author of the counsel by which Philadelphus obtained the books of the Jewish law; but others judge that Philadelphus never used the services of that Demetrius in furnishing his library; for Laertius writes that he was held in great hatred by that king, and accordingly died of the bite of an asp near Diospolis at the beginning of his reign. Nor could the renowned Vossius meet this testimony otherwise than by asserting that Philadelphus founded his library while his father was still reigning, against the common belief of all the ancients; and even so the wedge does not suffice for the knot; for Philadelphus pursued Demetrius with capital hatred long before his father's death. Furthermore, very many almost dare to swear that six men out of every tribe were sent by the high priest of the Jews to Philadelphus to complete that work; yet there are those who will not believe, even on oath, that so great a number of learned men out of every tribe was then living at Jerusalem. Some will have it that they were not so much interpreters as prophets; among whom at one time was Augustine, De Civitate Dei, lib. xviii. cap. xliii., of which no one could ever have persuaded Jerome. Justin affirms that they were distributed into seventy-two cells, and that each one finished the whole work separately, in such manner that they disagreed in the rendering of not a single word; Jerome pleasantly laughs at the figment; Serarius and others lend faith to the story after Justin; more, with Jerome, deny it. Some write that the whole version was finished in seventy-two days; others repudiate that frightful haste. There are those who write that they translated the Pentateuch only, as Aristeas, Josephus, Philo, and almost all the Talmudic doctors; Espieres, Morinus, and very many of the moderns, that they translated the whole Scripture of the Old Testament. Some determine that the Jews rejoiced greatly in that work; others, that on account of the undertaking they solemnly instituted a fast, and execrated the work itself. Philo asserts that the Seventy made their version not from the Hebrew verity but from the Chaldee Targum, and some of the rabbis assent to him; Cappellus, and those who owe everything to him in the controversy about the points, that it was from a Hebrew codex written without points. Certain men say that the high priest sent to Philadelphus a codex, if not written, at least adorned with golden letters for honor's sake; most of the rabbis cry out that a codex of that kind is profane, and fit for no use. Some affirm that the interpreters were furnished with the highest skill in the languages, and used the highest fidelity in executing the work; others, that they deliberately corrupted the sense in many places for various reasons; Munster and Ximenes, that they lacked either fidelity or understanding. Truly marvelous are the things Jerome writes of this version to Augustine. And to Paula and Eustochium, in the preface to Isaiah, he expressly affirms that the interpreters were unwilling to betray the sacraments of their faith perspicuously to the heathen. Very many conjecture that the Greek version which still exists under their name is the very one [ p. 379 ] which they elaborated of old; Drusius contends, and not alone, that the ancient one perished long ago. If it survives, very many concede that it has been most vilely corrupted in numerous places since Jerome, among whom is Bellarmine; Morinus asseverates that it has been preserved pure and inviolate. Some reckon the whole tradition of that version a Jewish fable, as we said before. Heinsius, in his Aristarchus, cap. x., determines that the fable-mongers snatched the handle for patching it together from Exod. 24:7, 11. "It is said," says he, "that Moses, Aaron, and the seventy elders went up to God, and that upon the princes of the children of Israel he laid not his hand; for which last words the Hellenists have, 'And of the chosen ones of Israel not even one was discordant' [Greek: Kai tōn epilektōn tou Israēl diephōnēsen oude heis]. Hence, no doubt, the history of Ptolemy and those seventy elders... hence those famous stage-scenes which the blessed Jerome hisses off, hence that invention that no one in so great a number disagreed in expressing the sacred things." The conjecture of that most learned man would perhaps have pleased those who are not ignorant how skilled the Jews are as craftsmen in fabricating the most portentous lies out of the slightest occasions, had not a more recent Aristarchus pronounced him, after he had ceased to be among the living, a grammarian and a vain man, deserving ridicule for his arrogance. To subject these divers opinions, and the other sword-fights about this version, to examination in this place would be foreign to the work in hand. But since it is commonly referred to that time of which we are treating, nothing, so far as I know, hinders me from saying briefly what I think of it.
III. That a version of the law or Mosaic Pentateuch was made into the Greek language about the times of Ptolemy Philadelphus, the testimonies of very many and a celebrated tradition establish. It is probable that the work was undertaken by the Alexandrian Jews for the sake of those who in that dispersion used the Greek language and were not learned in Hebrew. Whether some Targum, as some will have it, existed at that time is plainly uncertain. The greatest part of the people, certainly, used at that time the Syro-Chaldee dialect. There were therefore, perhaps, among them men who went before the Hellenists in this work of translating the Scriptures into the vernacular tongue. But what is said of this is mere conjecture, since no vestiges of any such labor survive. To the Pentateuch, in the course of time, was added what remains of the Scriptures of the Old Testament. That the whole work did not proceed from the same authors very many confess, and there exist certain indications of it. That the Targumic work was completed after the same fashion we shall presently see. But from whomsoever it proceeded, this is plain, that the work was undertaken and completed when the Jewish church was already rushing headlong to destruction; and from this it may be conjectured how much that version would be worth, if it still existed entire and uncorrupted. But whatever remains of that first Alexandrian version, Jerome himself everywhere shows that in his days it had been variously corrupted and interpolated. It would be well if from that time onward [ p. 380 ] the common Greek version which then existed had suffered no harm. However it came about, it cannot be denied that it now dissents from the Hebrew verity in innumerable places, and quite often gives words under which lies no tolerable sense; in expounding which, how vainly the interpreters labor there is no need to say; the thing itself is evident. Yet there are those who affirm that our Savior used that version, and so commended it to the church; just as a certain mass-priest wrote that he was accustomed to sing Masses. Of the writers of the New Testament this might seem capable of being affirmed with a greater show of probability. For many places of the Old Testament are recited in their writings in the same words which still stand in the Greek version, although very often they seem to differ in some things from the Hebrew. Very many have shown that this discrepancy is of words only, not of sense; some also, that the apostles really followed the Hebrew text where they commonly seem to have adhered to the Greek version. Most pleasing to me is the opinion of those who affirm that the Holy Spirit, the author of the whole Scripture, expounding his own mind at his own pleasure, carried over testimonies from the Old Testament into the New using the words that pleased him; and that the Christian copyists of the Greek version gradually inserted those very words into it.
IV. There is no lack of most powerful arguments on which the authors of this opinion rest. It will suffice me to point out one in this place, which I know not whether anyone has hitherto observed. It is well known that some testimonies are quoted in the New Testament somewhat obscurely, so that it is not easy for everyone to discern at once from what place of the Old they are taken. It is certain that some persons inserted several of these into the Greek text in places where formerly there was no trace of them, and from which they neither were taken nor could be taken. Let those words serve as an example which the apostle cites in the Epistle to the Hebrews, cap. 1:6, "And let all the angels of God worship him" [Greek: Kai proskynēsatōsan autō pantes angeloi Theou]. Since certain men did not know whence the apostle had borrowed these words, they thought fit to insert them in Deuteronomy cap. 32:43; and so after these words, "Rejoice, you heavens, together with him" [Greek: Euphranthēte ouranoi hama autō], they added, "and let all the angels of God worship him;" of which words there is in the Hebrew text neither palm nor footprint, nothing that could offer any handle for inserting them; and that we may understand the place to be interpolated, immediately after the violent intrusion of those words they repeat that "rejoice" [euphranthēte], so that the sense of the Mosaic discourse might thus proceed in due order. But that the apostle did not bring forward those words from that place is made manifest, besides the fact that the words of the Holy Spirit, in whose name he brings them forward, are not there, by the preface which he sets before the testimony. "When," he says, "he brings the firstborn into the world, he says" [Greek: Hotan eisagagē ton prōtotokon eis tēn oikoumenēn, legei]; but in that Mosaic context [Greek: periochē] there are no words about the bringing of the firstborn into the world, the unfolding of which is the direct scope of the Holy Spirit in Psalm 97, in which that testimony was really spoken.
## [ p. 381 ]

V. I shall add a single place, and that a most familiar one, from those words of Moses, Gen. 47:31, "And Israel bowed himself upon the head of the bed" [Hebrew: va-yishtachu Yisrael al-rosh ha-mittah]. The apostle shows that Jacob adored God leaning upon the top of his rod or staff; strong in faith, that is, though almost at the point of death. Those apostolic words crept into the Greek text, "And he worshiped upon the top of his staff" [Greek: Kai prosekynēsen epi to akron tēs rhabdou autou], as though, forsooth, the apostle had so translated those words of Moses; for that the Greek interpreters did not originally render mittah in that place by "staff" [rhabdos] is evident enough from this, that they constantly render that little word, repeated several times in the same history concerning the same person and matter, by klinē, that is, a bed, as may be seen, cap. 48:2, etc., cap. 49:33. But of these things, God granting, elsewhere.
# Digression III. On the Origin of the Targums, or Chaldee Paraphrases

I. To this interval of time, when the Jewish church was already rushing to destruction, very many assign the origin of the Chaldee paraphrases, or Targums [Hebrew: targumim], at least of some parts of them. Whether there were formerly more versions of the sacred Scripture into that language than survive has been made uncertain by what some of the ancients said about the Greek version of the Seventy being made from the Chaldee paraphrase. All the learned, Jews and Christians, confess that there never existed an entire paraphrase upon the whole Scripture, elaborated by one man or by several in the same time or age. There still survives upon the law or Pentateuch of Moses a threefold Targum. One of these is distinguished by the name of Onkelos; another by that of Jonathan Ben Uzziel; the third, for what cause I know not, is called the Jerusalem Targum. That which exists upon the historical books and the prophets bears the name of the same, or of another, Jonathan. That upon the books of Psalms, Job, and Proverbs is attributed to a certain Joseph the one-eyed, or the blind, whoever he was. Of plainly uncertain authorship are the fabulous and old-womanish commentaries which exist under the name of Targums upon Esther, the Song of Solomon, Ruth, Lamentations, and Ecclesiastes. The books of Ezra and Daniel never had a Targum, since from the beginning they were written partly in Chaldee. Elias Levita does indeed mention a Targum of Daniel in his preface to the Methurgeman; but the train of his discourse proves that by Targum he there understands the Chaldee language itself, not an ancient paraphrase; and accordingly he desires that a Chaldee grammar be drawn up according to the norm of the Targum of Daniel. That a Targum upon the books of Chronicles survives, Ravius indicated not long ago; and a copy of it having at last been obtained by the worthy Samuel Clarke, Architypographus of the University of Oxford (a man most worthy of a better lot for his manifold erudition), he is shortly to publish it; and thus the chain of paraphrases upon all the books of the Old Testament will be complete.

## [ p. 382 ]

II. All that is said among authors about the time at which these Targums were elaborated is most uncertain. Josephus, Origen, and Jerome, those most skillful searchers-out of literary monuments of this kind, are silent. Sixtus Senensis was of opinion that some Targum was known to the ancients under the name of the Syriac codex. That codex, when at last published (if indeed it has been published), shows itself to be a Christian work, sufficiently different from those Chaldee paraphrases of the Jews. There are those who suspect that this Jonathan the son of Uzziel was, on account of the equivalence of the names [Greek: isodynamia], the same as Theodotion, the well-known interpreter of the Scriptures, whom all the ancients mention. But there is no one who records that that proselyte of Pontus interpreted the Scriptures in Chaldee, whereas his Greek translation was very well known. The ancient Christians, therefore, seem to have been ignorant of all these paraphrases. Some accordingly judge that they were all written not only after the destruction of the city and temple, but even after the age of Jerome. For it is known that he spent his life in those very places where it seems impossible that the names and deeds of these sons of Pelops should have wholly escaped him. The hatred which subsisted between Christians and Jews, by reason of which neither people deigned to celebrate the achievements of the other, is pleaded by some as the cause of this silence. That the common crowd on both sides was so minded is credible. But what is this to Josephus, the most learned of the Jews, who expounded most diligently all the achievements of his nation, keeping far off the causes of wrath and partisanship against the Christians, and who weaves a sufficiently long and honorable narrative even of the Greek version? What is it to Jerome, the most curious searcher not only of all books but of all Hebrew traditions? Why these men made no mention of these paraphrases must have some other cause. But I do not wish to cling tooth and nail to this argument. It is certain that Jerome did not see all the books and all the literary monuments which existed in his age; for neither the weakness of human wit nor the brevity of this life suffices for so great a thing. Nor is it credible that he committed to writing everything he saw or knew. We have also shown above that the argument drawn from the silence of certain ancient writers is not fit to deprive the antiquity of books or letters of their credit. Let the reader consult Petrus Galatinus, Arcana Catholicae Veritatis, lib. i. cap. iii. pp. 7-9; Martinus Raymundus, Pugio Fidei, lib. i.; Wilhelm Schickard, Bechinat Happeruschim, p. 37; Buxtorf, De Origine Punctorum, p. 127; Joseph de Voysin, preface to the Pugio Fidei; Cocceius on Sanhedrin, p. 226; Buxtorf, Abbreviaturae, p. 120; Morinus, preface to the Greek Bible; Hoornbeek, preface to his Controversiae Judaicae; Hottinger, Philologia Sacra, lib. i. cap. iii., p. 259; Rivet, Isagoge ad Sacram Scripturam, p. 119.
III. Of them all I shall treat severally in few words. Among all the Targumists, if you regard the dignity of the work, the first place by the consent of all the learned is due to Onkelos. I know not whether the Jews are of the same opinion, who take such great delight in figments and lies, of which there is a smaller stock in him [ p. 383 ] than in the rest. But in what age he lived, and who he was, is not yet settled. Some judge that he was a proselyte, and lived in the days of Hillel and Shammai, that is, at the least a hundred years before the destruction of the city. To this conjecture subscribes Rabbi Azariah in Imre Binah, and the author of the book Zohar, col. 131: "Shammai and Hillel," it says, "did not teach Onkelos a word of the law until he was circumcised." Others make him a proselyte also, but the sister's son [or brother-in-law] of the emperor Titus, and so to have lived at the very time of the destruction. So the author of the Juchasin, p. 52. Which opinion Azariah expressly rejects in Imre Binah, relying on no other argument than that of the age in which, on the tradition of certain masters, he had affirmed him to have lived. Some suspect, from the similarity of the name, that this Onkelos was that Aquila who, as Jerome testifies, in a second endeavor translated the sacred Scriptures into the Greek language with strict exactness [Greek: kat' akribeian]. Others relate also that a certain Aquila, celebrated among the Jews, lived about those times, whom they think to have been this Onkelos. Since, therefore, nothing certain remains about the time in which he lived, anyone is free to opine whatever he pleases at his own discretion. But his Targum, whoever he was, is almost the only one among the extant Chaldee paraphrases worth reading. For it adheres to the text, very often rendering the words, and generally the sense, of the Holy Spirit, nor does it wander of set purpose into old wives' fables [Greek: mythous graōdeis], which is the solemn custom of all the rest. Led by this consideration alone, I conjecture that this paraphrast [Greek: paraphrastēs] lived before the destruction of the city; for from that time onward, every man of them, being utterly destitute of the Holy Spirit, trifled childishly and ridiculously. In what place or price the work stood among the more ancient Jews is wholly unknown; that some of the later ones made much of it is attested by the stitching together of that codex in which the Hebrew text itself and this paraphrase of Onkelos are written out in alternate verses; but that it was ever read publicly in the synagogues the Jews deny; nor is there any reason at hand why that should have been done, since from the last dispersion the Jewish common people was most utterly ignorant of the Chaldee language. But to judge that this translation, of uncertain author and uncertain origin, is of any price or authority where it differs from the Hebrew verity, is the part of none but madmen.

IV. He who turned the prophets (Daniel excepted) and the historical books into the Chaldee language is said to be Jonathan ben Uzziel. There is also extant, as we noted above, a Targum of the whole law ascribed to the same name. Concerning the time in which he lived, all that is brought forward is no less uncertain than what we have related of Onkelos. Very many of the Jews affirm that he was a disciple of Hillel; for to him and to Shammai, as the most famous masters of the sects, they delight to ascribe everything whose origin they do not know. They feign also that miracles were wrought from heaven about the beginning and the completion of this work. For they write that the beginning of the work was received by an earthquake, and by a bath qol [Hebrew: bat qol, "daughter of a voice"] thundering horribly against the author for profaning the Scriptures; its progress, by an approval given through fire falling from heaven [ p. 384 ] to consume the flies which by chance fell wandering on the parchment on which he wrote; and its completion, by the highest praises, not unlike those with which the image of Christ honored Thomas [Aquinas], crying, after he had finished the first part of the second, "You have written well of me, Thomas"; whether they believe it themselves is uncertain. Galatinus contends (bk. 1, ch. 3) that the same Jonathan who wrote the Targum on the prophets also translated the law paraphrastically [Greek: paraphrastikos]. He relates also that he himself had at that time seen a copy of that version, which was not made public until a full century afterward. And, what is a most certain proof that he reported in good faith what he had seen, he recites the opening words of that paraphrase just as they now stand in the printed copies. For copies of the Targum of Jonathan were of old very rare, nor could more than one or two be found among the Jews themselves in any province, as Elias Levita testifies in the preface to the Methurgeman. Rabbi Azariah also, in Imre Binah, ch. 9, affirms that the same Jonathan composed the translation of the Pentateuch and of the prophets. Those who are of another opinion rest chiefly on this argument, that it is established by most certain indications that that paraphrast of the law lived not only after the destruction of the city, but even after the completion of the Talmudic work, of which he makes express mention on Exod. 26:2; whereas that other, the prophetic one, lived before the coming of Christ, nay, as some will have it, a little after the return of the people from the Babylonian captivity. But by what weight of reasons they prove that that prophetic Jonathan lived in the times which lay between the return from Babylon and the destruction of the city, on which the whole force of the argument hangs, is not yet established. The testimony of the Jews, unaccompanied by any evidence of fact from circumstances, does not suffice for proof. There are also rabbis besides Azariah who, with Galatinus, assert that both works are the offspring of the same author. I do not wish to make this quarrel my own. The only thing that, with any show of reason, persuades that these Jonathans were different men is that the prophetic Targumist does not trifle quite so far as that most foul contaminator of the Pentateuch. What is to be thought of the whole work I will show in few words. In rendering the historical books he is modest; he rarely gives the text word for word, yet neither does he wander at will everywhere. Yet even in these there are places where he trifles so childishly and Talmudically that I can scarcely believe that a manikin of such audacity and ignorance ever set his hand to expounding the Scriptures before the apostasy of that church was complete. It wearies me to bring his most trifling ravings in here; let the reader make trial in the fifth chapter of Judges, and he will find that we have invented nothing. In just the same manner he carries himself everywhere in translating the prophecies of Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel. Wherever the sense of a passage seems a little more abstruse and recondite, especially if it exhibits anything of the person, grace, or office of the Messiah (although he gladly retains the name of Messiah, and even sometimes inserts it where it does not occur in the text), he twists it with depraved invented allegories to carnal and [ p. 385 ] legal senses, boldly and desperately, that is, Jewishly. Let the fifty-third chapter of Isaiah serve as an example; from it the reader may understand how the blind Jews twist themselves and the words of the Holy Spirit, that they may extricate themselves from that rabbinic torture-house of unbelief. Into the rest of the prophets, as if he had settled with himself nothing else than perpetually to stray from the true sense of the words, he everywhere mingles the most perverse allegories with unbridled license. Expositions of genuine truth "appear, swimming here and there in the vast deep" (Virgil, Aeneid 1.118).

V. There remains the exposition of the law, which you may truly call a wagon-load of lies. For I do not fear to affirm that more monsters of fiction are not to be found in the Mohammedan Koran itself than that impure artificer of lies has heaped together into that paraphrase. There is scarcely anything of the most putrid old songs among the most foolish Talmudists which that impostor of signal audacity has not carried into his patchwork. That dunghill book is plainly unworthy to occupy any place among the expositions, commentaries, or Targums of the law. And all this I would have understood as said also of that other one, commonly called the Jerusalem [Targum].

VI. That Joseph also, who is said to have written the paraphrase on the Hagiographa, was truly blind. If anyone desires a specimen of Jewish audacity, blindness, impudence, ignorance, and lying speech, let him go to this author. The whole book of Esther, from head to heel, is befouled with the most impudent lies and ridiculous fables. The Song of Solomon swarms everywhere with Talmudic follies, and the Talmud itself is commended by name at ch. 5:10; nor is anything done more reverently in Ecclesiastes. The paraphrase on Job is without doubt a patchwork, blown together out of the attempts of several, and it does not lack rabbinic ravings. The book of Psalms and the Proverbs of Solomon have felt the contaminations of the impure man less. Yet I would not say that there is plainly no use in these Targums, since they express cautiously the things that are said of God in the Scriptures after a human manner [Greek: anthropopathos], and do not twist to others the places that pertain to the Messiah, as is the manner of the more recent Jews (for the name of Messiah occurs more than fifty times among the Targumists, as Elias observes in the Methurgeman), although they miserably corrupt their sense; and he who wishes to become acquainted with the Jewish fables may learn them from these.
